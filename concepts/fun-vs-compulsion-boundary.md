# fun-vs-compulsion-boundary

## Definition

Whether a strong engagement-maintaining mechanic (variable-ratio reward) counts as fun or as retention-compulsion; response-rate data alone cannot tell.

## Why it matters here

- **hopson2001behavioral** — Hopson is hedonically neutral and disclaims highest-rate ≠ best.
- **johannes2021video** — Objective play time (N=3,274, industry telemetry) is small-but-positively, not negatively, related to well-being, and need satisfaction predicts well-being independently of play time — empirical counterweight to the "excessive playtime = harm" premise behind gaming-disorder policy.
- **ballou2024registered** — Preregistered registered report, N=414, 6-wave Xbox-network-wide telemetry (not one game): no practically significant within-person relationship between playtime and well-being in either direction at 24 hr/7 day/14 day timescales. Same population as `ballou2024basic`, where need satisfaction/frustration explains ~8.4% of this dataset's playtime variance (≈r=.3) — i.e. *why you play* tracks playtime far better than *how you're doing* does, sharpening the case that raw hours/session-return is the wrong proxy for either fun or harm.
- **ballou2025perceived** — Third independent platform-level dataset (N=703, 140k+ Nintendo Switch hours), null playtime→well-being at all 12 timescales from 1 hr to 1 yr; but self-reported "gaming life fit" (perceived value/interference across 5 life domains) predicted well-being with an effect an order of magnitude larger than any playtime coefficient — direct evidence that a short perceived-value self-report beats raw hours/session data as a fun-vs-harm proxy, though the authors flag a plausible reverse-appraisal confound (feeling bad → rating gaming as harmful) they cannot rule out.
