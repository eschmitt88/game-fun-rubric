# Why We Play Games: Four Keys to More Emotion Without Story

## TL;DR

XEODesign's 2004 field study of 30 players (+15 non-players) across ~40
commercial games used contextual inquiry, end-of-session questionnaires, and
observed facial/verbal emotional cues to catalogue over 30 distinct
gameplay-driven (non-story) emotions, consolidated into **Four Keys** —
Hard Fun, Easy Fun, Altered States (internal experience), and The People
Factor (social) — each keyed to a distinct emotion cluster. The paper's
headline design claim: analysis of best-sellers (Bejeweled, Halo, GTA,
EverQuest, Mario Kart: Double Dash) shows they each deliver emotion through
**at least three of the four keys**, not just one.

## Claims

- Games produce emotion through mechanisms other than narrative cutscenes;
  the study found "over thirty emotions coming from gameplay rather than
  story" (p.1).
- Four Keys, each a distinct route to emotion, were derived from ~12
  consolidated behavioral models built from the raw observations (p.3):
  1. **Hard Fun** — challenge, strategy, problem-solving → **Frustration**
     and **Fiero** (Italian: personal triumph over adversity — called "the
     ultimate Game Emotion," p.6). Player quotes: "playing to see how good
     I really am," "requiring strategy rather than luck" (p.3).
  2. **Easy Fun** — immersion via ambiguity, incompleteness, and detail →
     **Wonder**, **Awe**, **Mystery**/curiosity. Player quotes: "exploring
     new worlds," "wanting to figure it out," "feeling like me and my
     character are one" (p.4).
  3. **Altered States** — perception/thought/behavior/social context
     changing the player's internal state ("games as therapy") →
     **Excitement**, **Relief**. Player quotes: "clearing my mind by
     clearing a level," "being better at something that matters" (p.4–5).
  4. **The People Factor** — competition, cooperation, performance,
     spectacle → **Amusement**, **Schadenfreude**, **Naches**/Kvell
     (Yiddish: pride in a child's/mentee's accomplishment). Player quote:
     "it's the people that are addictive not the game" (p.5).
- **Best-selling games hit ≥3 of the 4 Keys.** "Analysis of best selling
  games such as Bejeweled, Halo, Grand Theft Auto (GTA), EverQuest, and
  Mario Kart Double Dash reveals that these games create emotion in at
  least three of the Four Keys… Combined these factors make a deeply
  enjoyable game for a wide market" (p.3). This is presented as *the*
  actionable design finding of the paper.
- Group play amplifies emotional display: "Players in groups emote more
  frequently and with more intensity than those who play on their own"
  (p.7) — an explicitly flagged surprise finding, not a headline claim.
- The Four Keys were selected from the ~12 candidate models using four
  explicit inclusion criteria (p.3): (1) what players like most, (2)
  creates emotion *without* relying on story, (3) already present in
  ultra-popular games, (4) has support in psychology theory (Ekman on
  emotion, Csikszentmihalyi on flow, Norman on affective/emotional
  design) — i.e., the taxonomy was curated for actionability and
  cross-validated against theory and market success, not derived by a
  blind clustering procedure.
- An emotion/trigger table (p.6) gives concrete design triggers, e.g.
  Fear ← "object moving quickly to hit player, sudden fall or loss of
  support"; Fiero ← overcoming a difficult obstacle, players raise their
  arms, "does not need to experience anger prior to success, but it does
  require effort"; Schadenfreude ← beating a long-term rival.

## Methods

- **Design**: exploratory field study, not an experiment; explicitly
  disclaims statistical significance or causal claims ("It is beyond the
  methodology and scope of this study to produce statistically
  significant results," p.2).
- **N**: 30 adult players (15 self-identified "hardcore," 15 "casual")
  playing their own favorite PC/console/handheld/internet games for 90–120
  minutes in their normal play environment (home, dorm, workplace, public
  gaming room); 4 sessions were 3–6-player console multiplayer, 2 were
  online PC multiplayer, the rest solo. A further 15 non-players (friends/
  family present during sessions) were separately interviewed about why
  they don't play — 45 distinct participants total.
- **Data collected**: 45 hours of video (verbal + non-verbal behavior),
  end-of-session written questionnaires, and researcher session notes,
  yielding "over 2,000 observations" coded from video transcripts,
  **facial expressions**, questionnaire responses, and notes, sorted into
  affinity groups (p.2). Method is XEODesign's proprietary "XEOAnalysis™"
  layered on standard Contextual Inquiry.
- **Emotion coding**: verbal and non-verbal (facial/body-language) cues
  reviewed from video, informed by (but not shown to strictly implement)
  Ekman's FACS-adjacent framework from *Emotions Revealed* — the paper
  does not describe a formal double-blind FACS coding protocol,
  inter-rater reliability check, or coding manual; "facial coding" here
  means researcher-reviewed video, not a validated automated/psychometric
  instrument.
- **Games sampled**: ~40 commercial titles spanning fighting, racing,
  puzzle, sports/strategy, and other genres (full table p.2) —
  deliberately cross-genre, which is directly useful for this project's
  genre-agnostic aim.
- This document is explicitly a preview: "This report contains highlights
  from XEODesign's upcoming work on 'Why We Play Games'" (p.2) — i.e. an
  abstract/highlights version of a fuller internal study, not the
  complete dataset or analysis.

## Results

- 30+ distinct gameplay-sourced emotions identified (headline number; not
  fully enumerated — the paper lists roughly a dozen with named
  triggers: Fear, Surprise, Disgust, Naches/Kvell, Fiero, Schadenfreude,
  Wonder, plus Frustration, Amusement, Excitement, Relief named in the
  Four Keys section).
- Best-sellers checked against the framework (Bejeweled, Halo, GTA,
  EverQuest, Mario Kart: Double Dash) each register on ≥3/4 Keys — no
  per-game breakdown table is given; this is a summary claim, not raw
  scored data.
- No quantitative statistics (no percentages, effect sizes, or
  significance tests) anywhere in the document — all results are
  qualitative pattern descriptions and player quotes.

## Critique / open questions

- **N=30 self-selected players playing their own favorite game** is a
  convenience sample with no control condition; findings describe *what
  emotions favorite games already produce*, not causal manipulation of
  which mechanics produce which emotion — useful as a taxonomy, weak as
  causal evidence for "install mechanic X to get emotion Y."
- No inter-rater reliability figure for the facial/verbal emotion coding,
  and "facial coding" is looser than the term implies (researcher
  judgment from video, not FACS-scored).
- XEODesign is a commercial player-experience consultancy selling this
  exact methodology ("XEOAnalysis™") to the client list printed at the
  bottom of the paper (Sony, Ubisoft, Mattel, Sega, …) — a whitepaper
  that doubles as a sales document has some incentive toward
  presenting findings as more decisive/actionable than a purely academic
  write-up would.
- Not peer-reviewed; no raw data, transcripts, or code released; citation
  count could not be verified (see frontmatter). Credibility rests on
  (a) an experienced, named industry group with a 12-year (at time of
  writing) track record and named major-studio clients, (b) explicit
  grounding in established psychology (Ekman, Csikszentmihalyi, Norman),
  and (c) the fact that this exact four-key framework is one of the
  hand-picked "core frameworks in scope" for this project per
  `CLAUDE.md` — i.e. it is being treated as foundational regardless of
  its non-peer-reviewed status, which is a reason to read it critically
  rather than to skip it.
- **Terminology drift warning**: this document (v.2004–2005) names Key 3
  "Altered States" and Key 4 "The People Factor." `docs/rubric.md`
  dimension 7's source line currently says "Lazzaro (Serious Fun)" —
  "Serious Fun" and "People Fun" are the names Lazzaro used in *later*
  talks/decks (post-2004), not in this whitepaper. The rubric's citation
  should either be corrected to match this primary source's terminology
  or explicitly note it's citing the later-renamed version of the
  framework (see Rubric implications).
- The paper is self-described as a "highlights" abstract of a fuller
  unpublished report — some claims (e.g. the exact "30+ emotions" count,
  the best-seller ≥3/4-keys claim) are asserted without the underlying
  per-game/per-emotion data table that would let a reader audit them.

## Rubric implications

- **5.5 (Fiero moments)** — **directly supported**. Hard Fun → Frustration
  → Fiero is exactly this criterion's mechanism; the paper's description
  of Fiero ("does not need anger prior to success, but does require
  effort," arms-raised behavioral marker) sharpens the anchor language.
- **6.3/6.4 (information gaps, experimentation rewarded)** — **supported**.
  Easy Fun's trigger set (ambiguity, incompleteness, detail → Wonder/Awe/
  Mystery/curiosity) is close to a mechanism statement for why
  information gaps and undirected exploration produce fun; useful added
  citation alongside Malone.
- **7.2 (Emotional range)** — **supported, and traceable**: the rubric's
  own 4-anchor exemplar list "dread, relief, awe, humour, triumph" maps
  almost one-to-one onto this paper's emotion table (Fear≈dread,
  Relief, Wonder≈awe, Amusement≈humour, Fiero≈triumph) — this source is
  the likely origin of that language and should be the cited anchor for
  7.2, not left implicit.
- **Dimension 7 source line / terminology fix** — the rubric cites
  "Lazzaro (Serious Fun)" for dimension 7, but this primary source calls
  the relevant key "Altered States" (Key 3) and "The People Factor" (Key
  4). Recommend `docs/rubric.md` either update the citation to name
  "Altered States" explicitly or add a footnote that "Serious Fun"/
  "People Fun" are this framework's later (post-2004) renaming — as
  written, the citation doesn't match what a reader will find in the
  cited primary source.
- **"Known gaps" / social dimension** — The People Factor (Key 4) is
  explicitly the social/multiplayer key. This confirms the rubric's own
  noted gap ("missing: social/competitive dimension, out of scope for
  now") is a real hole in coverage of this exact source, not an
  oversight — when a social dimension is added, this paper is the
  citation for it, and the paper's finding that group play *amplifies*
  emotional intensity (not just adds a new emotion type) is worth
  capturing then.
- **Proposed new criterion — "Key coverage check"**: the paper's most
  concrete, falsifiable design claim is that best-sellers hit ≥3 of 4
  emotion keys, not 1. Consider adding a lightweight cross-cutting
  check (not necessarily a new weighted row, could be a rubric-level
  gate or a note under "How to use") — e.g. "for each of Hard Fun / Easy
  Fun / Altered-States / People-Factor analogues already scattered
  across dimensions 5–7, can the designer point to a concrete mechanic
  that delivers it — and does the game clear at least 3 of 4?" This
  operationalizes a genre-agnostic emotional-breadth check that the
  current rubric doesn't explicitly test (dimensions 5/6/7 each test
  depth within one key-analogue, not breadth across them). Justification:
  it's the one quantitative-ish pattern in an otherwise purely
  qualitative source, and it's stated as the paper's own actionable
  takeaway for designers.
- **No support for G1/G2 (core loop, interesting decisions)** — this
  paper doesn't speak to loop or decision structure; no claim either
  way.
- **Caution on evidence weight**: given the critique above (N=30,
  no inter-rater reliability, commercial incentive, no raw data), treat
  the *taxonomy* (Four Keys + emotion catalogue) as reasonably durable —
  it's descriptive and consistent with later literature (Lazzaro's own
  later PXI/4Fun work, and general acceptance in game-design curricula)
  — but treat the **≥3-of-4-keys-in-best-sellers claim** as a
  design-evidence-quality flag: it is asserted, not shown with data, and
  should be scored as "designer opinion supported by unaudited pattern
  observation," not as an empirical result on par with, e.g., a
  controlled PENS/PXI survey study.

## Trust signals

- **Credibility: 3** — A reputable, long-running (12-year, at time of
  writing) industry player-experience research firm with named
  major-studio clients (Sony, Ubisoft, Mattel, Sega, LeapFrog), explicit
  grounding in established psychology theory (Ekman, Csikszentmihalyi,
  Norman), and a documented (if informal) qualitative methodology —
  balanced against: not peer-reviewed, no released data/code, no
  verifiable citation count, small self-selected sample, no inter-rater
  reliability reporting, and a commercial incentive to present the
  proprietary "XEOAnalysis™" methodology favorably. Net: solid-but-not-
  academic primary source; appropriate to cite as the canonical
  statement of the Four Keys framework (which is why relevance is high),
  but its specific quantitative-flavored claims (≥3-of-4 keys) should be
  flagged as unaudited when used to justify rubric weights.
