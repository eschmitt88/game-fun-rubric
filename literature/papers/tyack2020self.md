# Self-Determination Theory in HCI Games Research: Current Uses and Open Questions

## TL;DR

A systematic review of 110 CHI/CHI PLAY papers (2009–2019) that cite
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) finds that HCI games research treats SDT
mostly as a decorative vocabulary — competence/autonomy/relatedness and
SDT-based questionnaires (PENS, IMI) are widely used to *describe* the
player experience, but SDT's formal mini-theories (CET, OIT, etc.) are
almost never engaged with, key terms (especially "autonomy") are used
inconsistently across papers, and design recommendations attributed to SDT
are rarely theory-driven. This is not a study of what makes games fun; it
is a meta-scientific audit of how rigorously the games-research field
grounds its fun-adjacent claims in one of its most-cited motivational
theories — directly relevant to this project's evidence-quality standard.

## Claims

- SDT is a macro-theory of human motivation built on the satisfaction of
  three basic psychological needs — **autonomy** (sense that actions are
  self-endorsed and performed willingly), **competence** (feeling of
  having an effect), and **relatedness** (reciprocal care/belonging) —
  organized into six mini-theories (p. 2).
- The PENS scale (Player Experience of Need Satisfaction) has five
  subscales: competence, autonomy, relatedness, presence/immersion, and
  intuitive controls; independent validation studies generally support the
  factor structure but "it remains unclear whether intuitive controls and
  competence represent distinct subscales (particularly for experienced
  players)" and presence/immersion items #4 and #8 recur as problematic
  across studies (p. 2, citing unpublished personal communication).
- **Need density hypothesis**: people whose needs are poorly satisfied
  in daily life may be more likely to develop an unhealthy preference for
  the "dense, consistent, and immediate" need satisfaction available in
  videogame play — formulated to reconcile need-satisfying game qualities
  with dysfunctional play. "Applied work on the topic is limited" but
  "early findings ... have found some support" (p. 2).
- Across the 110-paper sample, only **3 papers** explicitly named and
  engaged with a specific SDT mini-theory (all three used Cognitive
  Evaluation Theory) — despite SDT formally being organized around six
  mini-theories with testable propositions (p. 4, "Engagement with
  mini-theories and key concepts").
- Competence was discussed in 84.55% of the sample vs. autonomy 65.45% and
  relatedness 57.27% — the authors read this asymmetry as reflecting
  competence's conceptual overlap with flow, dynamic-difficulty, and
  self-efficacy research rather than SDT centrality per se (p. 4).
- **82.73%** of papers engaged with SDT only *descriptively* (restating
  core tenets, often "curiorily," without positioning it in the work);
  **27.27%** used SDT *explanatorily* to motivate hypotheses/research
  questions; only **8.18%** used SDT to formulate concrete *design
  recommendations*, and even those were often generic ("theory offers
  little insight into identifying what will be motivating for an
  individual") rather than individually tailored (pp. 5–6, quoting [153]).
- **63.64% (n=70)** of reviewed papers employed at least one SDT-based
  measurement instrument. The IMI (Intrinsic Motivation Inventory) and
  PENS were each used in 40.00% of papers; within IMI use,
  interest/enjoyment was the most common subscale (40.00% of IMI users);
  within PENS use, competence (38.18%) and autonomy (36.36%) subscales
  dominated (Table 1, p. 6).
- The authors document *inconsistent operationalization of autonomy*
  specifically: some papers treat it as volition to engage in play at all,
  others as freedom of in-game choice/strategy selection, others cite
  vaguer definitions ("a sense of freedom and independence") that "outside
  of games, autonomy and independence were found to be empirically
  distinct" (p. 5).
- Extrinsic motivation was frequently and incorrectly conflated with
  *external regulation specifically* (its least self-determined form
  under Organismic Integration Theory), eliding OIT's graduated model
  (external → introjected → identified → integrated regulation, Fig. 2,
  p. 3) that includes more self-determined forms of extrinsic motivation
  (p. 5).
- The paper's central diagnosis: HCI games research treats SDT less as a
  falsifiable scientific theory and more as what Rogers [153] calls a
  "paradigm" — "a set of practices that a community has agreed upon...
  phenomena to be observed, and how findings ... are to be analyzed and
  interpreted" — i.e., a shared vocabulary rather than a predictive
  framework being actually tested (p. 8).
- Consensus is lacking on what PENS/IMI measures actually *capture*:
  "it remains unclear whether PENS measures of need satisfaction
  constitute PX [player experience] or rather its antecedents" (p. 9).

## Methods

- Systematic literature review of CHI and CHI PLAY proceedings (chosen as
  flagship HCI-games venues; explicitly excludes Computers in Human
  Behavior, IJHCS, Foundations of Digital Games, and non-HCI venues — a
  scope limitation the authors flag themselves).
- Scopus search for co-occurrence of "self-determination," "theory," and
  "game" across all paper sections → 166 results; extended abstracts
  (n=37, CHI PLAY only) removed automatically → 129 papers; a further
  n=19 excluded by both authors as lacking substantial relevance to
  either SDT or games → final N=110.
- Two coders (the paper's authors) developed coding categories from 6
  jointly-discussed seed papers, then split the remainder, met weekly to
  reconcile categories, and revisited earlier coding as categories
  evolved. Coding covered: venue, research domain, study type, SDT-based
  measures used, SDT concepts named, definitions given (if any), claims
  made about SDT, and purpose for citing SDT (descriptive / explanatory /
  design / conceptual — categories not mutually exclusive).

## Results

- N=110 papers, 2009–2019, with a sharp increase in SDT citations from
  2014 onward (coinciding with the launch of CHI PLAY and a jump in PENS
  usage) — Fig. 3, p. 4.
- 24 distinct research domains identified; *player experience* was the
  single largest category (61.82% of papers), followed by *design and
  interaction techniques* (21.82%) and *gamification* (18.18%) (p. 4).
- Purpose-for-citing breakdown (non-exclusive categories): descriptive
  82.73%, explanatory 27.27%, design-recommendation 8.18%, conceptual
  framework development ~2 papers (p. 5–7).
- Measurement breakdown: IMI 40.00% (n=44), PENS 40.00% (n=44), with 15
  additional papers using other SDT-adjacent instruments (UPEQ, PXI,
  BPNS, SIMS, GAMS, SRQ-E) — Table 1, p. 6.

## Critique / open questions

- This is a review of **research practice**, not a primary empirical
  study of player experience — it produces no new evidence about which
  design features increase fun. Its evidentiary value to this project is
  epistemic/methodological, not a direct data point for any rubric
  criterion's anchors.
- Scope is CHI/CHI PLAY only; the authors explicitly note this likely
  undercounts more rigorous SDT engagement published elsewhere (e.g.
  Foundations of Digital Games, Computers in Human Behavior) — so "SDT is
  used shallowly" should be read as "shallow in HCI-games flagship
  venues," not as a verdict on SDT-and-games scholarship overall.
- The paper is itself a caution against exactly the kind of citation this
  project should avoid: naming "SDT/PENS" in `docs/rubric.md` Dimension 2
  without specifying *which* SDT construct, *which* mini-theory, and
  *which* measure is meant. The rubric currently does this compactly
  ("SDT/PENS autonomy") — defensible as a design-time heuristic label,
  but per this paper's own recommendation, worth one added clause making
  explicit that "autonomy" as used in 2.1–2.5 is *behavioral/design-level*
  choice-availability (à la Deterding's contextual-autonomy-support model,
  cited p. 9) rather than a claim of validated PENS-autonomy-subscale
  correlation with fun.
- Distinguishes empirical finding from designer opinion clearly itself:
  the review explicitly flags where reviewed papers' claims about SDT
  were "dubious (and often uncited)" (p. 9) — a useful reminder that
  citing a famous theory's name is not the same as citing its evidence.

## Rubric implications

- **2 (Agency & meaningful choice, 15%, sourced to "SDT/PENS autonomy")**
  — SUPPORTS keeping the dimension: original SDT/PENS studies (cited
  within this review, not reproduced here) do link need satisfaction,
  including autonomy, to game enjoyment and persistence (p. 2, "need
  satisfaction has repeatedly been found to predict game enjoyment
  [135,166] and playing persistence [129]"). ADDS a caution: this paper's
  finding that "autonomy" is used inconsistently across the field
  (volition-to-play vs. in-game choice-density vs. self-congruence) maps
  onto real seams already present in 2.1–2.5 — 2.1/2.2/2.4 (decision
  density, trade-offs, multiple approaches) are *choice-availability*
  autonomy; 2.5 (player sets own goals) is closer to *volitional/
  self-congruence* autonomy. Recommend a short clarifying note in
  Dimension 2's preamble distinguishing these, citing this paper as the
  source of the distinction.
- **`docs/rubric.md` "Known gaps"** — directly answers the listed gap
  "Weights are designer folklore, not evidence. Look for empirical work
  on which PENS/PXI factors best predict enjoyment/retention": this paper
  does NOT itself supply that empirical link (it's a review of citation
  practice, not a meta-analysis of effect sizes) but it names the
  original studies to chase next (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006; the
  McAuley et al. IMI validation; Deterding 2016 on contextual autonomy
  support) — flag as a follow-up fetch rather than resolved here.
- **design-evidence-quality** (project-wide epistemic standard) — this
  paper is a strong worked example: it shows concretely how a rubric or
  design guideline can misuse a real, well-evidenced theory by citing its
  name without its structure. Proposed use: when future literature notes
  invoke "SDT" for any criterion, require a one-line note on which SDT
  construct/mini-theory/measure is meant, per this paper's critique.
- No direct evidence bearing on Dimensions 1, 3–8 (mastery, flow, feel,
  goals, novelty, narrative, clarity) — this paper doesn't touch them.
- No proposed new criterion or weight change. Proposed *wording* change
  only: add one sentence to Dimension 2's preamble in `docs/rubric.md`
  distinguishing choice-availability autonomy (2.1/2.2/2.4) from
  volitional/self-congruence autonomy (2.5), citing Tyack & Mekler 2020 —
  left for a human/ingest-owner decision since this agent may not modify
  `docs/`.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 4** — strengthens the evidentiary basis for an existing
  load-bearing rubric dimension (2, Agency & meaningful choice) by
  clarifying what "SDT/PENS autonomy" should and shouldn't be taken to
  mean, and supplies a citable epistemic caution (design-evidence-quality)
  that generalizes across the whole rubric. Does not itself seed a new
  concept or supply primary effect-size evidence, so short of a 5.
- Chase the primary sources this review names for the actual
  need-satisfaction → enjoyment/persistence effect sizes: Ryan, Rigby &
  Przybylski 2006 (original PENS paper); Przybylski, Rigby & Ryan (the
  Motivational Pull framework); Deterding 2016 (contextual autonomy
  support in games) — the last is flagged in this paper as the most
  rigorous existing model of *in-game* autonomy and a good next fetch.
