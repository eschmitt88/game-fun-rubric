# Measuring Perceived Challenge in Digital Games (CORGIS)

## TL;DR

Perceived challenge is not one thing: across two large surveys (N=394 EFA,
N=987 CFA) the authors factor-analyse a 60→30-item pool down to a validated
4-factor scale — **Cognitive, Emotional, Performative, and Decision-Making
challenge** — and show the four subscales discriminate between three
structurally different games exactly as hypothesised (N=148 held-out
players, all p<.001, η²=.57–.63). The paper does **not** itself test or
report a challenge↔enjoyment correlation; that link is asserted from prior
literature in the intro/discussion and explicitly flagged as future work.

## Claims

- Challenge and difficulty are conceptually distinct: "difficulty" =
  objective probability of task failure (citing Lomas et al. 2017);
  "challenging" = the felt, relational, valence-laden player experience of
  that difficulty (p.5). CORGIS measures the latter, and specifically
  challenge *originating from the player's most recent gameplay session*
  (temporal framing chosen because perceived challenge depends on
  accumulated skill, p.5).
- Four distinct, empirically separable factors of challenge (p.26–27):
  - **Cognitive (COG)** — planning, memorisation, multitasking, effort.
  - **Performative (PERF)** — speed/accuracy of physical action.
  - **Emotional (EMO)** — emotional salience, character attachment, moral
    material; *not* resolved by skill/dexterity, only by processing the
    narrative.
  - **Decision-Making (DM)** — a genuinely **novel factor not previously
    named in the challenge literature** (p.35–36): difficult/regrettable
    choices. Historically lumped into "cognitive challenge"; the authors
    argue it deserves separation because it behaves oppositely to
    performative challenge (quick reaction vs. deliberate choice) and
    covaries strongly with emotional challenge (shared "weight of a
    decision" component).
  - A single-factor ("challenge is unidimensional") model was tested and
    explicitly rejected in the confirmatory study: PERF items had near-zero
    loading on a general factor (below 0.25) and a confirmatory bifactor
    analysis found no PERF items and only some EMO items loading on a
    general factor at all (p.29–31).
- COG and PERF are nearly orthogonal to each other; PERF and DM are
  slightly **negatively** correlated (r=−0.21 in the CFA sample, p.31,
  Table 6) — i.e., games/moments that push rapid action and games/moments
  that push deliberate difficult choices are, in players' felt experience,
  somewhat opposed, not additive.
- EMO and DM correlate strongly (r=0.56–0.65 across both studies) — the
  authors interpret this as decision-making challenge carrying much of the
  "weight" of emotional challenge (e.g., choosing which character to
  sacrifice), not as poor discriminant validity (convergent/discriminant
  validity stats support keeping them separate — AVE > MSV for all four
  factors, Table 7, p.32).
- Section 2.2's literature synthesis (secondary, not this paper's data)
  catalogues hypothesised challenge→other-experience links: challenge→flow
  (Chen 2007), challenge→immersion specifically via time pressure raising
  performative+cognitive challenge (Cox et al. 2012, cited as empirical),
  challenge→competence (SDT, Ryan et al. 2006), challenge→affect/enjoyment
  (Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi 2012), and a claim that players' preferred
  difficulty rises over a play session (Klimmt et al. 2009; Bateman et al.
  2011). These are explicitly **not new evidence generated by this paper**
  — CORGIS deliberately excludes items overlapping uncertainty, immersion,
  and competence "as these are covered in dedicated instruments" (p.21).
- Deliberately excludes a "social challenge" construct: interview data
  suggested social play amplifies the meaning of other challenge types
  rather than constituting a distinct challenge dimension in itself (p.20).

## Methods

- **Stage 0 (conceptualisation):** literature review across games user
  research, design, and AI, synthesised into a working definition (p.12,
  boxed): "Perceived Challenge: a player experience that arises from one's
  interaction with a game's intrinsic challenges at a particular skill
  level."
- **Stage I (qualitative):** semi-structured interviews, N=9 (4 players +
  5 games-user researchers), open coding/thematic analysis → generated 120
  candidate items → trimmed to 60 covering emotional/cognitive/physical
  challenge plus performance-evaluation and difficulty-management themes.
- **Stage II (EFA):** online survey, 60 items, 7-point Likert, recent-game
  recall. N=432 collected → N=394 retained after removing high-missingness
  respondents. 57 women / 332 men / 5 non-binary, mean age 25.9 (SD 7.2),
  mean 18.2 years gaming (SD 6.8). Used **Item Response Theory (IRT)**, not
  classical CTT factor analysis (mirt R package, multidimensional IRT,
  Direct Oblimin rotation) — chosen because Likert items are ordinal, not
  continuous (p.22, explicit methodological argument). KMO MSA=0.92, all
  item MSA>0.7. Scree/parallel analysis suggested ~5 factors; single-factor
  solution explained only a "modest" coherence (43/60 items loaded >0.35
  but only 2 items >0.7). 4-factor solution chosen over 5/6-factor
  alternatives for simple structure and conceptual coherence (a 5-factor
  split of cognitive challenge correlated r=−0.38 with itself, judged
  redundant). Result: 30-item reduced pool, α = 0.88 (COG, 11 items), 0.86
  (EMO, 9 items), 0.93 (PERF, 5 items), 0.84 (DM, 5 items).
- **Stage III (CFA):** second online survey, N=1001 collected → N=987
  retained (83 women / 874 men / 30 non-binary/unspecified, mean age 24.6,
  SD 6.48, mean 17.5 h/week playing, SD 6.70). CFA sample N=839 (combining
  held-in halves of three specific games' players + other respondents).
  KMO MSA=0.93. 4-factor CFA model (items forced to 0 loading outside their
  factor) explained **81.6% of variance**. Composite reliability (CR),
  Average Variance Extracted (AVE), Maximum Shared Variance (MSV) computed
  per Fornell & Larcker (1981) criteria: CR 0.88–0.96, AVE 0.47–0.81 (all
  ≥0.5 except EMO at 0.47, "just below" threshold), AVE > MSV for all four
  factors (Table 7) → convergent + discriminant validity supported.
- **Stage IV (criterion validity):** N=148 players held out from Stage III,
  split across three games with a priori hypothesised challenge profiles:
  *Life is Strange* (narrative adventure, n=36), *Monster Hunter World*
  (real-time action RPG, n=47), *XCOM* (turn-based tactics, n=65).
  One-way ANOVA per subscale across the three games.

## Results

Table 8 (M±SD per game, ANOVA all p<.001, N=148):

| Subscale | Life is Strange | Monster Hunter World | XCOM | F(2,145) | η² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cognitive | 3.59±1.29 | 5.62±0.78 | **5.97±0.53** | 96.8 | 0.57 |
| Emotional | **6.21±0.84** | 3.43±0.82 | 4.60±0.76 | 123.3 | 0.63 |
| Performative | 3.07±1.56 | **6.24±0.82** | 2.67±1.37 | 116.0 | 0.62 |
| Decision-Making | **6.28±0.88** | 3.33±1.42 | 6.12±0.83 | 115.1 | 0.61 |

All four subscales moved in the hypothesised direction with large effect
sizes (η²=0.57–0.63) — e.g., a narrative choice-driven game (Life is
Strange) scored highest on Emotional and Decision-Making challenge and
lowest on Performative; a real-time action game (Monster Hunter World)
inverted that pattern. One surprise: Monster Hunter World's Cognitive
challenge (5.62) was nearly as high as XCOM's (5.97), which the authors
attribute to strategic depth in its real-time combat not anticipated by
the a priori hypothesis (p.34–35) — a caution that the hypothesised
game→challenge-type mapping is directional, not exact.

The paper explicitly cautions against over-reading this validation: many
other confounded features (aesthetics, control complexity, language)
differ between the three games, and "it would take many more carefully
controlled studies to provide firm evidence that the differences in the
experience of challenge seen here are indeed due to the hypothesised
challenges in the gameplay" (p.34).

**No enjoyment measure was collected or correlated with the four subscales
anywhere in this paper.** The relationship between perceived challenge and
enjoyment is stated as a target for future work in both the Discussion
(§9) and Conclusion (§10): "additional empirical studies need to be
conducted to explore the relationship between perceived challenge and
other player experiences, including immersion, uncertainty, autonomy and
competence" (p.37) and "we are confident that this scale is an essential
tool that will assist researchers and game developers in learning more
about perceived challenge and its relationship to player enjoyment,
**ultimately leading to better games**" (p.38, forward-looking, not a
result).

## Critique / open questions

- **The challenge↔enjoyment link the intro promises is never tested.**
  The abstract/intro build considerable motivation around challenge being
  "widely believed to play a crucial role in making games enjoyable," but
  the paper's own contribution is purely psychometric (does the
  4-factor structure hold, does it discriminate known-different games) —
  not a nomological/criterion validity test against enjoyment, PENS
  competence, flow, or immersion, despite the instrument's items being
  deliberately built to *exclude* overlap with those constructs. This is
  an appropriately scoped Stage-1 scale-validation paper, but it should
  not be cited as evidence that any particular challenge type *drives*
  enjoyment — only that the four types are statistically real and
  discriminate real games.
- EMO's AVE (0.47) falls slightly below the conventional 0.5 threshold,
  and one item ("Playing the game was stimulating") consistently
  underperforms (item-drop r=0.32–0.39 across both studies, low factor
  loading 0.35) — the authors keep it but flag it as marginal.
  Decision-Making and Emotional challenge share considerable variance
  (r=0.56–0.65); the authors argue this is a real "weight of the
  decision" phenomenon and not a discriminant-validity failure, but the
  MSV values for EMO (0.42) and DM (0.42) in Table 7 mean up to 42% of
  variance in each is shared with the other — a reader should treat
  "Decision-Making challenge" as most cleanly isolated when it does *not*
  also carry heavy narrative/moral weight.
- Sample skew: both surveys recruited via Twitter/Reddit gaming
  communities and skewed heavily male (84–89%) and young (mean 24–26,
  high self-reported weekly play hours, 17.5 h/week) — a self-selected,
  high-engagement gamer population, not a general audience. Generalisation
  to casual/non-enthusiast players is untested.
- The Stage IV validation used only 3 games and non-independent samples
  (held out from the same recruitment pool as the CFA), so it is closer to
  "the scale doesn't obviously fail" than an independent generalisation
  test; the paper is candid about this (p.34, "it is important to be
  cautious in this sort of analysis").
- No test-retest reliability, no cross-game/cross-difficulty-setting
  sensitivity test (explicitly named as next-step future work, p.37) —
  so CORGIS's usefulness for **within-game** difficulty-tuning
  (comparing two builds of the same game) is unvalidated as of this paper.
- Methodologically strong choice to use IRT over classical CTT factor
  analysis for ordinal Likert data (well-justified, p.22), and the
  decision to run a genuinely independent confirmatory sample (N=987,
  not a re-analysis of the same N=394) is good practice relative to a lot
  of games-research psychometrics (the rubric's own S3 calls out
  GameFlow's weaker, non-independent validation as a cautionary example).

## Trust signals

- **Credibility: 4** — peer-reviewed at IJHCS (Elsevier, established HCI
  venue), reputable UK games-research group (Cairns is a long-standing
  player-experience/GUR researcher; York and City both have active games
  research groups), pre-registered-style two-stage EFA→CFA design with
  genuinely independent large samples (N=394, N=987, ~1400 total
  participants), 110 citations in ~6 years (Semantic Scholar,
  2026-08-25), supplementary materials (full game list) released via DOI.
  Not a 5 because: no scoring script/analysis code released (only a game
  list), IJHCS is solid but not top-tier-flagship HCI (CHI/TOCHI), and one
  factor (EMO) has a borderline AVE.

## Rubric implications

*(rubric v0.2, docs/rubric.md)*

- **3.1–3.4 (Challenge–skill balance & flow, functional, E2/E3/E1 mix):**
  Strong supporting evidence that "challenge" as scored by the rubric is
  itself multi-component, not a single dial. Denisova et al.'s DM↔PERF
  near-zero/negative correlation (r=−0.21) and the Monster Hunter
  World/Life is Strange/XCOM profile divergence (Table 8) is direct E2
  evidence that a game can independently max out one challenge type (3.1's
  "irregular wave") while flatlining another — a design implication the
  current single-scale 0–4 anchors for 3.1–3.4 don't surface. **Proposed
  addition:** a sub-note under 3.1 recommending raters score challenge
  calibration *per type* (cognitive/emotional/performative/
  decision-making) when the game mixes genres, citing
  denisova2020measuring's four validated, largely-independent factors as
  the operational basis (E2) — currently 3.1's evidence tier rests only on
  PXI Challenge (E2) and Chen/Falstein theory (E4); this paper adds an
  independently-derived, larger-N (N=1400) empirical taxonomy.
- **1.1 (Depth and breadth of pattern space, "≥2 mechanic types," currently
  E4 via Koster's four mechanic types):** CORGIS's four factors (COG,
  EMO, PERF, DM) are an *empirically factor-analysed* (E2) alternative
  challenge-type taxonomy to Koster's E4 four mechanic types
  (heuristic problem-solving, theory-of-mind, physical/autonomic,
  probability estimation). They don't map 1:1 but overlap substantially
  (PERF≈physical/autonomic, COG≈heuristic problem-solving, DM≈a
  decision-specific split the rubric doesn't currently distinguish from
  cognitive load). **Proposed change:** cite denisova2020measuring
  alongside koster2012theory in 1.1's evidence column as E2 corroboration
  that "breadth across mechanic types" is a real, separable player
  experience, not just Koster's designer taxonomy — this would let 1.1
  cite mixed E2/E4 rather than E4 alone.
- **2.2 (Trade-offs, not puzzles — currently E5, "not operationalised by
  PXI Autonomy"):** This paper's discovery of Decision-Making challenge as
  a *statistically distinct, novel factor* (not previously named in the
  challenge literature, CR=.89, AVE=.63) is the closest existing
  operationalisation of "meaningful/weighty choice" as a measurable player
  experience the graph currently has. **Proposed evidence-tier upgrade:**
  2.2 from E5 to E3 — DM is empirically validated as a felt experience
  distinct from cognitive challenge, though it measures the *presence and
  weight* of difficult choices, not specifically "trade-off between goods
  vs. right/wrong answer," so it corroborates rather than directly proves
  2.2's specific framing.
- **7.2 (Emotional range, currently E3):** EMO factor items (character
  responsibility, real-life-issue resonance, moral dilemmas, sadness) and
  its huge, game-specific swing (6.21 in Life is Strange vs 3.43 in
  Monster Hunter World, η²=0.63) is solid E2 evidence that narrative/moral
  weight registers as a distinct, measurable "challenge," supporting 7.2's
  premise that emotional range is design-controllable and detectable, not
  just designer intuition (E3→ arguably E2 for the narrower claim that
  emotionally-loaded content is measurably distinguishable across games).
- **Does NOT support any claim in the rubric about challenge→enjoyment
  causality or correlation.** Nothing here should be cited as evidence for
  or against 3.x's psychosocial-mediation framing
  (vandenabeele2020development's PXI model) or for 5.5's fiero claims —
  this paper deliberately did not measure enjoyment. If a future ingest
  wants that link, it needs a different source (the paper's own citations
  — Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi 2012, Klimmt et al. 2009 — are candidates
  to fetch directly rather than borrowing secondhand from this paper's
  lit review).
- **No proposed new dimension/weight change** — this paper strengthens
  evidence tiers on existing criteria (1.1, 2.2, 3.1, 7.2) rather than
  motivating new ones. The clearest structural suggestion is the 3.1
  per-type calibration note above.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 5** — seeds a new concept (validated multi-factor challenge
  taxonomy) and supplies E2 evidence directly usable to upgrade three
  existing rubric criteria's evidence tiers (1.1, 2.2, 3.1) plus
  corroborating evidence for a fourth (7.2); also directly usable as the
  "companion validated instrument" the rubric's own "How to use" step 4
  recommends pairing with rater scores (currently only PXI/PENS are
  named — CORGIS is a more specific fit for scoring dimension 3).
- Fetch a challenge→enjoyment paper directly rather than relying on this
  paper's secondary citations (candidates named in its lit review:
  Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi 2012 "Enjoying the possibility of defeat,"
  Klimmt et al. 2009 on preferred-difficulty-rises-over-session, Cox et
  al. 2012 on time pressure → immersion).
- Consider fetching Bopp et al. 2018 (the emotionally-challenging-games
  survey this paper repeatedly cross-validates against) as a second
  emotional-challenge source.
- CORGIS itself (the final 30-item scale, Table 5) could become a
  concrete measurement appendix if the project ever runs its own
  playtests — it's short enough (30 items, 7-point Likert) to administer
  alongside the rubric's blind-rater protocol (S3).
