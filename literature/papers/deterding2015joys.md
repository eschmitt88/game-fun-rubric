# The Joys of Absence: Emotion, Emotion Display, and Interaction Tension in Video Game Play

## TL;DR

An empirical (qualitative interview) grounding of Erving Goffman's sociological
theory of "euphoric ease": everyday face-to-face interaction requires constant,
effortful self-monitoring of conduct and emotion display ("interaction
tension"); games are unusual because their social norms often *permit and
demand* exactly the emotions players spontaneously want to express, so that
self-regulation effort disappears from awareness. Deterding reanalyzes 19
interviews (German adult gamers) and finds Goffman is broadly right about the
*norms* (players report a rich, nuanced set of "feeling rules" for emotion
display even in "silly," non-serious play) but wrong about the *phenomenology*:
players never spontaneously recalled the absence of self-regulation as
positive — except in solitary play, where freedom from emotion-display work
was explicitly and repeatedly described as a positive experience of
"freedom." The paper reframes euphoric ease as a conditional **hygiene
factor**, not a driver, of enjoyment — present-but-invisible when working,
noticeably absent (as "distraction," reduced immersion) when broken by an
audience.

## Claims

- **Theoretical setup** (§2, p.2): Goffman's "interaction order" is held
  together by two mechanisms — embarrassment (internalized fear of social
  disapproval that regulates conduct) and involvement (the socially contagious
  degree of attentive/emotional absorption permitted in a situation).
  "Dysphoric tension" is the normal state of everyday life: a mismatch between
  what one spontaneously wants to be involved in and what is situationally
  proper, requiring constant self-monitoring. Gameplay is unusually often
  "euphoric" because games are purpose-built "engrossables" whose social norms
  happen to align with what they spontaneously make players want to feel and
  express — "shared, obligatory, spontaneous involvement" (Goffman, quoted
  p.2).
- **Method** (§3, pp.2-3): Episodic interviews (Flick) + directed qualitative
  content analysis, grounded-theory constant comparison and theoretical
  sampling. **N = 19 interviews, avg. 90 min (~1,900 min total audio), German
  adult gamers**, deliberately sampled to include "canonical" leisure players
  alongside "atypical" ones (e-sports players, gameplay-as-work journalists/
  designers/researchers) to foreground taken-for-granted norms by contrast.
  Coded in MAXQDA v10; this paper is a **re-analysis** of 222 passages
  originally coded "emotion"/"interaction tension" in a larger study (not a
  new data collection run for this specific question) — the author flags this
  explicitly as a limitation (no fresh saturation pass for the focal question,
  §5.1).
- **Finding 1 — three feeling rules for emotion display, even in "non-serious"
  competitive play** (§4.1, pp.3-4): (a) don't seriously hurt another's
  feelings or let display be mistaken for literal aggression; (b) don't
  derail others' enjoyment (a "spoilsport" who shows no involvement, or a
  "sore loser" who burdens others with frustration, both breach norms); (c)
  signal that intense emotion is contained/reversible — credibly "not
  serious," e.g. via laughter/irony — so the player's biographical self stays
  separable from the in-game outburst. A breach example given: a colleague
  who said "I grew *myself* as a person" from an in-game achievement was read
  by the interviewee as an inappropriate "flooding out" of the player-role
  boundary (P4/484).
- **Finding 2 — euphoric ease is a hygiene factor, not a remembered positive**
  (§4.2, pp.4-5): "none of their memories of positive experience were
  connected to the perceived *absence* of emotional self-regulation" — but
  players readily recalled cases where forced self-regulation *impeded*
  enjoyment (e.g. a player on a train: "I am somehow in flow... and immersed
  and in there, so I still catch myself... can't focus on the game one
  hundred percent," P10/403).
- **Finding 3 — the one exception is solitary play** (§4.2, p.4-5): the only
  reports of freedom-from-regulation as a *positive* experience in its own
  right came from solitary play. A game designer interviewee contrasted
  playing alone at home ("I can simply show all emotions... that's not a
  feeling of freedom [at the office]... I would enjoy it less") vs. playing
  with friends on a couch (considerateness for others dominates) vs. the
  office (professional competitor-analysis context). Another player, asked
  about feelings to control on a solo iPad session: "No. There I am by
  myself. I have no contact with others and when I don't want to anymore, I
  stop" (P17/738-740). A third reported swearing loudly at strangers in
  anonymous online play but not doing so with friends physically present
  (P19/89-96) — anonymity, not just solitude, lowers the regulation bar.
- **Finding 4 — the shielding is socio-material, not purely normative**
  (§4.3, pp.5-6): mobile/portable play is Deterding's "breaching experiment"
  showing that freedom from regulation depends on a *setting* — private
  space (home bedroom/living room: physical shielding + legal privacy norms)
  and/or a legitimizing public frame (transit/waiting contexts — trains,
  subways, doctors' waiting rooms — license visible "subordinate
  involvement" like gaming or reading; a classroom does not). One player
  physically repositioned himself in a train's two-seat row to create a
  visual "blind" (P10/361-363) and reported the awkwardness of his mother
  overhearing his cryptic StarCraft VoIP callouts in the kitchen (P10/221-237)
  as a concrete instance of interaction tension breaking through even in a
  quasi-private setting.
- **Contextualization / theoretical linkage** (§5.2, pp.6-7): Deterding
  explicitly connects the "freedom" language players used to
  **Self-Determination Theory's autonomy need** — perceiving oneself as
  acting autonomously/self-congruently is inherently satisfying; feeling
  controlled/coerced (including by internalized social display norms) thwarts
  it, "exactly what Goffman described as 'dysphoric tension.'" He also lists
  candidate psychological mechanisms for *why* interaction tension is
  unenjoyable: cognitive dissonance, goal conflict, and — most relevant to
  flow/concentration research — a "necessary constant attention switching
  between main task and self-regulation, reducing full attentive absorption"
  (p.7).

## Methods

Qualitative, non-experimental: episodic interviews (Flick 1997) with a
semi-structured script built from Goffmanian frame-analysis categories
(settings, objects, roles, emotion, rules, boundaries, etc.); directed
qualitative content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon 2005); grounded-theory constant
comparison/theoretical sampling to saturation (Corbin & Strauss); coded in
MAXQDA 10. This specific paper is a targeted re-analysis (not fresh
collection) of a subset of codes from a larger 19-interview study on social
norms of German adult video game play. No quantitative measures, no
statistical tests, no control condition — purely interpretive/theory-building.

## Results

No numeric results (qualitative study). Key structural results: 3 feeling
rules for emotion display (regard for other's feelings; regard for other's
enjoyment; credible containment of the biographical self); a reported
absence of positive euphoric-ease memories in *social* play contrasted with
its presence specifically in *solitary* play; and identification of two
necessary socio-material preconditions for euphoric ease (a legitimizing
social frame + physical shielding from onlookers), independent of whether
play is nominally "solo."

## Critique / open questions

- **Small, culturally homogeneous, non-representative sample** (N=19, German
  adults) — the author states this explicitly (§5.1) and disclaims
  generalizability or statistical reliability. This is squarely an E3-tier
  qualitative study: theory-grounding, not theory-testing.
- **Re-analysis, not a purpose-built study**: the data was originally
  collected for a broader study of gameplay social norms, then recoded for
  this specific question. The author is transparent about this but it means
  no fresh theoretical-saturation pass was run for the "euphoric ease in
  solitary play" claim specifically — it emerged from patterns in
  already-collected material, which is weaker evidence than a study designed
  to test it.
- **No independent construct validation**: "freedom" as used by interviewees
  is the author's own bridge to SDT autonomy — a plausible but *post hoc*
  interpretive link, not measured with a validated instrument (e.g., no PENS
  administered alongside the interviews).
- **Directly useful negative-space framing for a fun rubric**: this is one of
  very few sources in the graph arguing that an *absence* (of a demand, a
  cost, a friction) can itself be a positive design lever, distinct from
  "add more positive stuff." That maps cleanly onto the rubric's Dimension 8
  framing ("clarity & friction... usability removes fun when absent and adds
  none when present" — a subtractor, not a contributor) — Deterding supplies
  an independent, differently-sourced argument for the same subtractor
  logic, but for *social/emotional* friction rather than interface friction.
- **Directly on-scope for this project**: because the rubric is explicitly
  scoped to *single-player* design, Deterding's central empirical finding —
  that solitary play is the one context in which freedom from emotion-display
  labor was reported as a positive experience — is a specific, load-bearing
  data point in favor of treating single-player as its own design space with
  distinct affordances, not "multiplayer minus social features."

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 4** — this paper doesn't anchor an existing rubric dimension
  the way a meta-analysis or validated-instrument paper does, but it supplies
  a genuinely new mechanism (emotion-display labor as a hidden cost,
  solitude/privacy as the release valve) that (a) reinforces the SDT-autonomy
  evidence already load-bearing for Dimension 2, (b) adds an attention-cost
  explanation directly relevant to Dimension 3's concentration/workload
  criterion, and (c) is unusually on-topic for a project explicitly scoped to
  *single-player* design — it is one of very few sources in this literature
  that treats solitary play as analytically distinct rather than as a
  degenerate case of multiplayer. Not 5, because it's a single small
  qualitative study proposing a hygiene-factor reframing rather than
  supplying validated, generalizable measurement or an ablation.
- Read Deterding's 2014 dissertation "Modes of Play: A Frame Analytic Account
  of Video Game Play" (the source corpus) if a deeper dive into the feeling-
  rules taxonomy across genres/modes is ever needed.
- The companion citation "Contextual Autonomy Support in Video Game Play"
  (Deterding, CHI 2016) looks like a natural quantitative follow-up to check —
  worth a future `/discover` pass.
- Tyack & Mekler's `tyack2020self` (already in this project's graph) is the
  natural cross-reference for the SDT-autonomy construct-consistency question
  this paper's §5.2 gestures at but doesn't resolve.

## Rubric implications

- **2.5 Self-directed play (autonomy, E2)** — supported/deepened, no weight
  change proposed. Deterding supplies an E3 qualitative mechanism for *why*
  autonomy predicts enjoyment (ryan2006motivational β=.76): being free of
  imposed emotion-display/self-monitoring labor is explicitly experienced by
  players as "freedom," Deterding's own bridge to SDT autonomy-need
  satisfaction. This is corroborating theory, not new quantitative evidence —
  keep 2.5's tier at E2 (driven by ryan2006motivational), cite deterding2015joys
  as a supplementary mechanism note, not a tier upgrade.
- **3.4 Concentration and workload (E2/E3)** — minor supporting evidence.
  Deterding's candidate mechanism ("constant attention switching between main
  task and self-regulation, reducing full attentive absorption," p.7) is a
  plausible, citable explanation for *why* interruptive social/self-
  presentation demands break flow — directly parallel to 3.4's "no dead time,
  no unimportant tasks" anchor, just applied to an *internal* task (emotion
  regulation) rather than external interruption. Worth a citation add, not a
  score/weight change.
- **8.x Clarity & friction (subtractor framing)** — structural parallel, no
  criterion change proposed. Dimension 8's framing ("usability removes fun
  when absent, adds none when present") is exactly Deterding's "hygiene
  factor" logic, independently derived from a completely different
  literature (Goffman/interaction-order sociology vs. SDT/usability). This
  strengthens confidence in the *subtractor* pattern as a general design
  principle, worth noting in `docs/decisions/` if the rubric's authors want
  to generalize the subtractor framing beyond Dimension 8 specifically — not
  something this note is authorized to edit into `docs/rubric.md` directly.
- **Proposed NEW consideration, not a scored criterion**: the rubric
  currently has no criterion capturing *play-context/audience* as a design-
  adjacent lever (e.g., does the game assume/encourage private, uninterrupted
  solitary sessions vs. impose spectator-visible scoreboards, forced
  voice/video chat, or streaming overlays during single-player play?). This
  is genre-agnostic and single-player-specific, but it's more a *deployment/
  UX-context* variable than a *core-loop* design variable, so it may not
  belong as a numbered criterion at all — flagging as a candidate for
  `docs/decisions/` discussion rather than proposing rubric text here. One-line
  justification: Deterding's single positive-euphoric-ease report from
  solitary play (vs. none from any social-play condition) suggests
  designers of single-player games get a "free" enjoyment floor from privacy
  that companion apps, always-online modes, or forced social features can
  erode — worth a designer-facing note even if not worth a scored row.
- **No contradiction found** for any existing criterion. This paper does not
  argue against any current rubric row; it adds an emotion-regulation/
  audience-context mechanism orthogonal to the functional→psychosocial
  gating structure already in place.
