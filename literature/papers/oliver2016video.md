# Video Games as Meaningful Entertainment Experiences

## TL;DR

Experimental/correlational study (N = 512, online sample) asking players to
recall either a particularly *fun* or particularly *meaningful* game, then
rating game characteristics, need satisfaction (PENS: competence, autonomy,
relatedness, plus a new researcher-created "insight" need), and enjoyment vs.
appreciation. SEM shows two largely separate predictive tracks: **gameplay
quality → competence/autonomy → enjoyment** vs. **story quality →
relatedness/insight → appreciation**, with insight the single strongest
predictor of appreciation (β = .54, p < .001). Meaningful games are recalled
less often than fun games (71.9% vs. 97.6% of participants could name one)
but are rated just as enjoyable — meaningfulness does not trade off against
fun.

## Claims

- Games are broadly able to deliver both hedonic (enjoyment) and eudaimonic
  (appreciation) entertainment experiences, and these are empirically
  separable outcomes with different predictors, not opposite ends of one
  scale (Results, "Manipulation Checks" and Figure 2).
- A 2×2 mixed ANOVA on recalled fun vs. meaningful games found enjoyment was
  high in both conditions (fun M = 6.70 vs. meaningful M = 6.57 on a 7-point
  scale, p < .05, small gap) but appreciation was substantially higher for
  meaningful-game recall (M = 5.96) than fun-game recall (M = 5.25, p < .001).
  I.e., meaningfulness "does not appear to detract from... enjoyment, but it
  does serve to boost feelings of appreciation" (Results section).
- RQ1: fun games are recalled far more readily than meaningful ones —
  97.6% of participants in the fun condition could name a game vs. 71.9% in
  the meaningful condition, χ²(1, N=512) = 64.22, p < .001, Cramér's V = .35
  — but meaningful recollection is "far from uncommon."
  N=432 (out of 512) were included in the enjoyment/appreciation manipulation
  checks; N=512 in the full recall/CFA/SEM analyses.
- A confirmatory factor analysis of 12 need-satisfaction items (3 items each
  for competence, autonomy, relatedness, and a new construct the authors
  label **insight** — "playing this game has given me new insights about
  life," "...has made me wiser," "...has enriched my life in some way")
  supported four distinct-but-correlated factors (CFI = .96, RMSEA = .06);
  constraining relatedness and insight to a single factor significantly
  worsened fit, Δχ²(1) = 246.39, p < .001 — insight is measurably distinct
  from relatedness even though the two correlate strongly (r = .70).
- Final structural model (N=512, adequate fit: χ²(104)=230.60, p<.001,
  CFI=.96, RMSEA=.05) — key standardized path coefficients (Figure 2, Discussion):
  - Game Play → Competence Need: β = .34, p < .001
  - Game Play → Autonomy Need: β = .35, p < .001
  - Game Play → Enjoyment (direct): β = .15, p < .05
  - Story → Relatedness Need: β = .56, p < .001
  - Story → Insight Need: β = .31, p < .001
  - Competence Need → Enjoyment: β = .26, p < .001
  - Autonomy Need → Enjoyment: β = .21, p < .001
  - Relatedness Need → Appreciation: β = .13, p < .05
  - Insight Need → Appreciation: β = .54, p < .001 (by far the strongest
    predictor of appreciation in the model)
  - Recalling a "meaningful" (vs. "fun") game → Story rating: β = .33, p<.001
  - Recalling a "meaningful" game → Insight Need, direct path (beyond the
    story-mediated route): present and significant, suggesting unmeasured
    mediators beyond story quality also drive insight/appreciation.
  - Recalling a "meaningful" game → Game Play rating: β = −.01, ns.
  - R² : Competence Need .10, Autonomy Need .15, Relatedness Need .31,
    Insight Need .19, Enjoyment .23, Appreciation .41.
  (Note: two path values visible in the figure, .13** and .21***, could not
  be unambiguously assigned to specific edges from the low-resolution figure
  scan; all coefficients above are transcribed directly from clearly legible
  arrows and the accompanying prose in the Discussion, which restates the
  gameplay→competence/autonomy→enjoyment and story→relatedness/insight→
  appreciation structure explicitly.)
- Nonsignificant paths were trimmed from the final model: relatedness →
  enjoyment, insight → enjoyment, competence → appreciation, autonomy →
  appreciation. I.e., the two need clusters (competence/autonomy vs.
  relatedness/insight) predict *only* their own outcome (enjoyment vs.
  appreciation respectively), not both.

## Methods

- Between-subjects experiment: participants randomly assigned to recall
  either a "particularly fun" or "particularly meaningful" video game
  (open-ended, undefined terms — participants supplied their own construal).
- N = 512 recruited from gaming websites, forums (PlayStation/Xbox
  Community, GameSpot, Gameinformer), social media, and professional
  listservs (Communication Research Theory Network) + snowball. 67.8% male,
  age 18–56 (Mdn=27, M=28.98, SD=7.44), M=7.47 years gaming experience
  (SD=6.41). Incentive: lottery for $50 Amazon gift cards.
- Measures: single-item 7-point enjoyment/appreciation ratings (adapted from
  Oliver & Bartsch 2010 but stripped of the words "fun"/"meaningful" to
  avoid confound with the manipulation); affect checklist (EFA → 3 factors:
  meaningful affect [touched/moved/compassionate/inspired], negative affect,
  positive affect) as a manipulation check; single-item 0–100 slider ratings
  of "gameplay" and "story" quality (styled like a GameSpot user review);
  PENS scale (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006) for competence/autonomy/
  relatedness (relatedness items reworded to reference in-game *characters*
  rather than other players, since a single-player game could be named);
  3 new researcher-authored items for "insight."
- Analysis: mixed ANOVAs for manipulation checks; CFA for the 4-factor need
  structure; two-step SEM (measurement model then structural model) per
  Kline (2005), with model modifications (correlated residuals, two added
  direct paths, four trimmed nonsignificant paths) documented and justified
  post hoc — i.e., partly exploratory/modified-post-hoc SEM, not a strict
  confirmatory test of a pre-registered model.

## Results

See Claims above for the full coefficient set. Headline results: (1)
enjoyment and appreciation are dissociable outcomes with almost entirely
non-overlapping predictor sets in the final model; (2) "insight" survives as
a statistically distinct 4th need beyond PENS's three, and is the dominant
predictor of appreciation; (3) meaningful-game experiences are less common
to recall than fun ones but not rare (72% recall rate), and do not come at
the cost of enjoyment.

## Critique / open questions

- **Causality**: authors explicitly flag this — the survey is
  cross-sectional/retrospective-recall, so temporal/causal ordering between
  game characteristics, need satisfaction, and enjoyment/appreciation cannot
  be established. They call for lab replication with manipulated game
  mechanics.
- **Recall bias / demand characteristics**: participants self-selected which
  game counts as "fun" or "meaningful" with no operational definition
  provided, and then rated that same game's characteristics — single-source,
  single-timepoint self-report throughout, so common-method variance likely
  inflates the fit statistics and path coefficients.
- **"Appreciation" here is not true afterglow**: it is measured in the same
  sitting as recall, immediately after describing the game — it captures
  retrospective meaningfulness judgment, not a persisting post-play state.
  Directly relevant caveat for rubric criterion 7.5, which already flags
  that PXI's Meaning construct is in-play rather than afterglow; this
  paper's appreciation measure has the same limitation, just applied
  post-hoc to a recalled game rather than in-play.
- **Post-hoc model modification**: the final SEM (Figure 2) was reached
  by adding two paths and trimming four nonsignificant ones from an initial
  poorly-fitting model (initial CFI=.86 → final CFI=.96) — a legitimate but
  exploratory procedure; the reported fit indices describe a model partly
  shaped by the same data used to test it, so should be read as descriptive
  structure, not confirmed theory.
- **"Insight" as a construct is intentionally content-free** ("insight about
  what?" is explicitly left unanswered) — this makes it easy to endorse but
  also somewhat under-specified as a design lever; it says players *feel*
  wiser/enriched, not what game mechanic produced that feeling beyond "story
  quality," and the direct (non-story-mediated) Meaningful-Game → Insight
  path in the SEM says a meaningful chunk of the variance in insight is
  driven by something the study didn't measure at all.
- **Sample is not representative**: recruited from gaming communities and
  research listservs; skews toward engaged/dedicated players (avg. 7.5
  years of gaming experience), not casual or non-gamers.
- Distinguishes empirical result from designer opinion cleanly throughout —
  this is a data paper, not a design-theory piece, and reads as one; the
  Discussion's design implications ("Heavy Rain," GTA narrative shift) are
  explicitly framed as interpretation, not further evidence.

## Rubric implications

Reading against `docs/rubric.md` v0.2:

- **7.5 Meaning / afterglow (E2 partial → can cite as additional E2
  support).** This is the closest direct hit in the rubric: oliver2016video
  gives a validated, large-N (512), SEM-tested empirical link from *story
  quality* → relatedness/insight need satisfaction → appreciation
  (β=.54 for insight, the single strongest path in the whole model). Add
  oliver2016video as a citekey alongside the existing PXI-partial citation.
  Caveat carried over: like PXI Meaning, this study's "appreciation" is
  measured in the same sitting as recall, not true post-play afterglow — the
  7.5 anchor's "players think about it when not playing" is still
  unvalidated by any source in the graph.
- **7.1 Fantasy fulfilment / 7.4 Self-expression (E2/E4).** Weak, indirect
  support: story quality driving relatedness (closeness to characters)
  bears on 7.1's "distinctive fantasy delivered by mechanics" only loosely —
  this paper measures story *quality*, not fantasy specifically. Not worth
  a citekey addition here.
- **2.5 Self-directed play (E2, PENS Autonomy).** Direct support: Game Play
  → Autonomy Need (β=.35) → Enjoyment (β=.21) replicates the PENS-autonomy→
  enjoyment link the rubric already cites via ryan2006motivational, in an
  independent N=512 sample. Reinforces the existing E2 tier; doesn't change it.
- **Structure section ("functional dimensions gate psychosocial
  ones").** Partial complication worth flagging in `docs/rubric.md`'s
  Known-gaps or Structure prose: this paper's data argue for **two parallel
  tracks** rather than one funnel — gameplay-type functional attributes feed
  competence/autonomy → enjoyment, while narrative-type attributes feed
  relatedness/insight → appreciation, and the trimmed paths show these
  tracks do *not* cross (competence/autonomy do not predict appreciation;
  relatedness/insight do not predict enjoyment). If the rubric's psychosocial
  dimensions (2, 5, 6, 7) are meant to gate through a single "functional
  floor" as currently written, this source suggests dimension 7 in
  particular (narrative/meaning) may run on a functionally *different* input
  (story quality) than dimensions 1/3/4/8 (gameplay quality) — i.e., a low
  gameplay-functional floor might suppress enjoyment/dims 1-2 without
  equally suppressing narrative-driven appreciation/dim 7. Proposed
  refinement (not made here, since this note may not edit docs/rubric.md):
  consider whether dim 7's psychosocial gating should draw on a
  narrative-specific functional check rather than the pooled functional
  subtotal.
- **Framing note ("games whose primary goal is... story delivery...may
  correctly score low without being bad").** oliver2016video is good direct
  support for this framing sentence — it empirically shows meaningful/
  story-driven games are not lower in enjoyment but do route through a
  different, non-overlapping predictor set than "fun" games. Candidate
  citekey to add next to koster2012theory there.
- No proposed new criterion or weight change — this paper's contribution is
  about a *second outcome variable* (appreciation) the rubric deliberately
  scopes out ("This rubric measures fun, not design quality"), so its main
  value is as evidence for the framing/gating prose and as an additional E2
  anchor for 7.5, not as a source for a new scored row.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance:** 4 — Directly informs the evidence status of criterion 7.5
  and the rubric's functional→psychosocial gating claim with a second,
  independent large-N SEM (beyond vandenabeele2020development); also
  substantively supports the "not-fun-but-legitimate" framing note. Not a 5
  because its main outcome (appreciation/meaning) is explicitly out of the
  rubric's core "fun" scope, so it strengthens framing/context more than any
  single scored criterion.
- Worth a follow-up ingest of the original Oliver & Bartsch (2010)
  enjoyment/appreciation dual-process paper this study builds directly on,
  and of Tamborini et al. (2010, 2011) SDT-gaming-enjoyment work, if not
  already in the graph — both are cited here as the direct theoretical
  ancestors of the SEM structure and are not currently present.
