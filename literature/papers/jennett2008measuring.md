# Measuring and Defining the Experience of Immersion in Games

Full text obtained as a genuine open-access PDF (44-page author manuscript, not the
final journal-paginated version) hosted on co-author Paul Cairns's University of York
staff page (`www-users.york.ac.uk/paul.cairns/pubs/JennettIJHCS08.pdf`) — the
publisher (Elsevier/ScienceDirect, DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2008.04.004) paywalls it. Content
matches the published abstract and reference list; page numbers cited below are
manuscript pages, not the journal's 641-661 pagination.

## TL;DR

Three experiments define and operationalize "immersion" in games as a graded,
moment-in-time experience distinct from flow, cognitive absorption, and presence —
develop and factor-analyze a 32-item Immersion Experience Questionnaire (IEQ) —
and find that immersion correlates with an objective behavioral cost (slower
task-switching back to reality) and a measurable eye-movement signature (decreasing
fixation rate), while also correlating with **negative** affect (state-anxiety), not
just positive affect: immersion is not a purely pleasant experience.

## Claims

- Immersion is conceptually distinct from three related constructs the field
  conflates it with (Section 2, pp. 5-8):
  - **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi) is a particular *extreme* optimal experience with 8
    strict components (clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge-skill balance,
    etc.); immersion is graded and doesn't require all of them. Counter-example:
    *Myst IV* has no clear goals and delayed feedback yet can be highly immersive;
    conversely a boss fight beyond the player's ability (a flow-breaking failure)
    can still feel immersive (p. 6).
  - **Cognitive Absorption** (Agarwal & Karahana 2000) is a stable *trait/attitude*
    toward IT in general; immersion is a *state*, tied to one specific play session
    (p. 6-7).
  - **Presence** is a state of mind (feeling of "being there" in a virtual world);
    the authors argue a double dissociation exists — Tetris involves no felt
    presence but can still be immersive (time loss, tuning out surroundings), while
    one can feel present in a VR sim doing a boring task without losing time-sense
    (p. 7).
- Three-level graded model of immersion, adopted from Brown & Cairns (2004)'s
  grounded-theory interview study (N=7 gamers), each level gated by a different
  barrier: **Engagement** (barrier: investment of time/effort/attention, gamer
  preference) → **Engrossment** (barrier: game construction — features combine so
  emotions are affected and controls become "invisible") → **Total Immersion**
  (barrier: empathy + atmosphere — sense of presence, "cut off from reality,"
  rare and fleeting) (pp. 4-5).
- **Experiment 1** (N=40, London university students, between-subjects: Half-Life
  FPS vs. a minimal button-clicking control task, 10 min each) — immersion score
  (32-item IEQ) was significantly higher in the game condition (M=69.6, SD=18.2)
  than control (M=52.5, SD=17.2; Mann-Whitney U=96, p=.005). Task-switch cost:
  participants completed a tangram (fox figure) task before and after; in the game
  condition only, the *reduction* in tangram time was negatively correlated with
  immersion score (Spearman's ρ=-.45, p<.005) — i.e., the more immersed, the less
  a person improved on returning to a real-world task. Regression: a 1-point rise
  in immersion score corresponded to a 4.2s smaller improvement. No such
  correlation existed in the control condition (p. 13-14).
- **Experiment 2** (N=41, independent-measures, Tobii 1750 eye tracker, same
  game/control tasks) — mean fixations per second *increased* over time (5-10 min
  into task) in the non-immersive clicking condition (Spearman's ρ=.518, p<.001,
  R²=26.4%) but *decreased* over time in the immersive Half-Life condition
  (ρ=-.191, p<.05, R²=4.3%) — read as attention narrowing/focusing ("zoom lens,"
  Styles 1997) under immersion vs. becoming distracted under boredom. Self-reported
  immersion was again significantly higher in the game condition on both a single
  10-point item (M=6.3 vs 3.9, U=89.5, p<.005) and the 32-item measure (M=66.0 vs
  44.9, U=60, p<.001); the two immersion measures correlated with each other
  (Spearman's ρ=.724, p<.001) (pp. 18-24).
- **Questionnaire validation** (separate online sample, N=244 of 260 respondents,
  94.3% male, recruited from top-10 gaming forums/games) — PCA + direct oblimin
  rotation on a revised, simpler-worded 31-item IEQ yielded **5 factors explaining
  49% of variance** (KMO=.845): *Cognitive Involvement*, *Real World Dissociation*,
  *Challenge*, *Emotional Involvement*, *Control* — a mix of "person factors"
  (the first, second, and fourth) and "game factors" (challenge, control) (pp.
  25-27).
- **Experiment 3** (N=36, 9 per cell, independent-measures) manipulated *pace* of
  interaction with the clicking task across 4 conditions (slow externally-paced /
  self-paced / fast externally-paced / increasing-paced) and measured the revised
  IEQ, State-Anxiety (Spielberger STAI), and PANAS-X. Immersion differences across
  pace conditions were in the predicted order (increasing 90.33 > fast 86.22 >
  slow 78.78 > self-paced 74.78) but **not statistically significant**
  (Jonkheere-Terpstra J-T=302.5, p>.05). **State-anxiety was significantly higher
  in the fast/increasing conditions than the slow condition** (J-T=359.5, p=.001);
  increasing-pace produced the single highest anxiety of all four conditions.
  **Negative affect** (PANAS-X) differed significantly across conditions
  (Kruskal-Wallis χ²(3)=13.512, p<.01), with increasing-pace significantly higher
  than slow (critical difference=16.821 > critical value 12.912). Positive affect
  did not differ significantly across pace conditions but **did correlate with
  overall immersion score** (Spearman's ρ=.711, p<.001) (pp. 27-31).
- **The headline finding, stated in the abstract and repeated in General
  Discussion (Section 18.1, p. 32)**: *"immersion is not only viewed as a positive
  experience: negative emotions and uneasiness (i.e. anxiety) also run high."*
  The authors explicitly contrast this with Csikszentmihalyi's claim that flow
  involves a "serene mindset" — immersion, by their data, is "much more
  emotionally charged" (p. 31) and can co-occur with high state-anxiety and
  negative affect, particularly under externally-imposed, escalating time
  pressure.
- Limitations the authors flag themselves: Experiment 3's clicking task, even at
  its most game-like (increasing pace), was still perceived by participants as a
  task rather than a "game" — absent graphics/sound/narrative — likely explaining
  the non-significant immersion result there (p. 31); the sample in Experiments 1-2
  mixed gamers and non-gamers without controlling for gaming experience, which the
  qualitative analysis of Experiment 2 suggests matters a great deal (P17's prior
  Half-Life experience vs. P19's "I just didn't get it") (p. 22-24, 33).

## Methods

- Mixed qualitative-groundwork + three quantitative experiments + one large-N
  psychometric validation, run across ~2005-2007 (UCL Interaction Centre /
  University of York / Bunnyfoot Behavioural Research Consultancy collaboration).
- Exp. 1: between-subjects, N=40, dual-task paradigm (game/control → tangram
  reconstruction task) measuring both self-report (32-item IEQ, negative/positive
  paired items to control wording effects) and objective task-switch cost (time).
- Exp. 2: independent-measures, N=41, Tobii 1750 eye-tracker (double
  computer/double screen), fixation-rate time series (300-600s window) plus the
  same 32-item IEQ; supplemented by a qualitative gaze-replay comparison of the
  4 highest- vs. 4 lowest-immersion participants per condition.
- Questionnaire refinement + validation: 31-item revised IEQ (6 subsections:
  basic attention, temporal dissociation, transportation, challenge, emotional
  involvement, enjoyment), online survey, N=244 self-selected gamers, PCA with
  direct oblimin rotation, Cattell scree method for factor retention.
- Exp. 3: independent-measures, N=36 (9/cell), 4 pace conditions (slow/
  self-paced/fast/increasing), Spielberger State-Anxiety Inventory + PANAS-X as
  affect measures, Jonkheere-Terpstra tests for the ordered directional
  hypotheses, Kruskal-Wallis for affect.
- All stats: SPSS 11.0, predominantly non-parametric (Mann-Whitney, Spearman,
  Jonkheere-Terpstra, Kruskal-Wallis) given no assumption immersion scores were
  normally distributed (though the authors note Exp. 1's data happened to fit a
  normal distribution and t-tests gave identical p-values).

## Critique / open questions

- **The core "immersion causes task-switch cost" claim (Exp. 1) is correlational
  within a manipulated-condition design, not fully causal at the individual
  level**: the authors themselves note the effect might reflect unmeasured
  confounds correlated with both game condition and immersion score rather than
  immersion per se (p. 14) — appropriately hedged, but worth flagging since it's
  the paper's most "objective" claim.
- **Experiment 3's null result for immersion across pace is arguably the most
  important finding for a design rubric**, not a limitation to explain away:
  manipulating pace alone, on a bare clicking task, could shift *anxiety* and
  *negative affect* significantly without moving self-reported *immersion*
  significantly. This is evidence against assuming that "make the interaction
  faster/more demanding" is itself a lever for immersion or fun — echoes
  caroux2023player's broader finding (already in this graph) that many
  individually plausible design levers don't survive controlled testing.
- **Gender skew in the validation sample** (94.3% male, N=244, self-selected from
  gaming forums) limits how far the 5-factor IEQ structure generalizes; the
  authors don't discuss this limitation explicitly.
- **Sample overlap with gamers vs. non-gamers not controlled** in Experiments 1-2
  (flagged as future work by the authors, p. 33) — the qualitative gaze analysis
  in Exp. 2 (P17's prior game experience, P12's exploratory play style) suggests
  prior familiarity is a real confound on immersion scores, not just an ignorable
  nuisance variable.
- **This is squarely an E1/E2-tier source by this project's own evidence-tier
  standard**: two controlled experiments with random assignment and significance
  testing (Exp. 1, Exp. 3 — though Exp. 3's key hypothesis was null) plus a
  large-N (244) factor-analytic validation (Exp. 2's questionnaire refinement) —
  substantially stronger evidentiary footing than most sources already in this
  graph (koster2012theory, lazzaro2004why, burgun2015why are E3-E5 designer
  theory/opinion).
- The paper is explicit that this is early-stage, exploratory work (Section 18.2)
  and does not claim the IEQ is a finished, universally validated instrument —
  a candor that strengthens rather than weakens its credibility.

## Follow-up

- The 5-factor IEQ structure (Cognitive Involvement, Real World Dissociation,
  Challenge, Emotional Involvement, Control) is worth comparing directly against
  vandenabeele2020development's PXI factor structure already in this graph — both
  instruments have a "Challenge" and something like a "Control" factor; whether
  they measure the same latent construct or something adjacent is an open
  question for `docs/decisions/` if the project ever needs to pick one instrument
  for its own playtest protocol.
- Brown & Cairns (2004) "A grounded investigation of game immersion" (CHI 2004)
  is the qualitative source of the 3-level engagement/engrossment/total-immersion
  model this paper builds on — not yet in `raw/`, would be a natural next fetch
  if the graded-immersion-levels idea gets promoted to its own concept.
- Sweetser & Wyeth's GameFlow (already ingested as sweetser2005gameflow, the
  rubric's own structural ancestor) is explicitly discussed and partially
  contrasted here (Section 2.1, p. 6): this paper argues GameFlow's notion of
  "immersion" bundles in player skill, clear goals, and feedback — factors this
  paper's authors hold are more properly *flow* factors, separable from
  immersion proper. Worth a cross-reference note if `docs/rubric.md`'s lineage
  field is ever revisited.

## Rubric implications

- **3.4 Concentration and workload (E2/E3, PXI Immersion / GameFlow
  Concentration)** — this paper is a second, independent, large-N (N=244)
  factor-analytically validated immersion instrument (IEQ) predating PXI by 12
  years and vastly more cited (2035 vs. vandenabeele2020development's smaller
  citation base). Its "Cognitive Involvement" and "Real World Dissociation"
  factors map closely onto what 3.4 is trying to score. Proposed addition:
  cite jennett2008measuring alongside vandenabeele2020development as a second
  E2 anchor for 3.4.
- **New caution for the scoring anchors and S2 ("score twice") setup**: the
  paper's headline finding — immersion correlates with *both* positive affect
  (ρ=.711, p<.001) and elevated state-anxiety/negative affect under demanding
  pacing (fast/increasing conditions significantly more anxious than slow,
  p=.001) — complicates any assumption that high engagement/concentration
  scores (3.4) straightforwardly imply a *pleasant* experience. This is
  directly relevant to **3.2 (Failure cost is calibrated)** and **G2
  (interesting decisions)**: a design that produces strong engagement via
  anxiety/pressure is not automatically "working" in the way a 4/4 on 3.4 would
  imply. Recommend a note in `docs/rubric.md`'s Dimension 3 preamble (not made
  here — flagging for the user/`docs/decisions/`) that concentration/immersion
  scores should be read alongside an affect check, not assumed positive by
  default.
- **Caution against "pace/speed = immersion" as an implicit design shortcut**:
  Experiment 3 found manipulating externally-imposed pace alone did *not*
  significantly move self-reported immersion (J-T=302.5, p>.05), even though it
  did move anxiety and negative affect significantly. Relevant to **4.1 (Input
  responsiveness)** and **3.1 (Difficulty tracks skill)**: speeding up
  interaction is not, by itself, evidenced to produce immersion/engagement —
  consistent with caroux2023player's already-cited finding that most
  individual "obvious" design levers don't survive controlled testing pooled
  or singly. No weight change proposed; this reinforces the existing "Known
  gaps" caution about difficulty/pacing rather than adding a new one.
- **No changes proposed to weights.** This source strengthens the evidentiary
  citation base for Dimension 3 (adds an E2 instrument citation) and adds a
  qualifying caution rather than a new criterion. Proposed new concept
  `immersion-not-purely-positive` (not yet a concept file — proposal only,
  per this note's scope) would be the natural home for the negative-affect
  finding if/when the user promotes it.
