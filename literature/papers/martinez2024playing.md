# Playing on Hard Mode: Accessibility, Difficulty and Joy in Video Game Adoption for Gamers with Disabilities

## TL;DR

A semi-structured interview study with 13 gamers with disabilities maps
"game adoption" — the whole process of finding, vetting, and getting set
up with a game, not just playing it — onto three phases: **Discovery**
(finding a candidate game), **Evaluation** (assessing whether it's
accessible/fit), and **Adaptation** (learning to play it and building
workarounds), the last a phase the authors argue prior HCI work
under-explores. The paper's theoretical payoff is the concept of **access
difficulty** — inaccessibility reframed *as a form of difficulty*,
produced by a mismatch between a player's abilities and the abilities a
game assumes, existing *between* player and game rather than as a fixed
property of the game — and **disabled gaming**, the broader practice of
navigating and creatively exploiting that mismatch (deviant play, hacking,
metagaming). Central finding for this rubric: accessibility functions as a
**gate that must be passed before a game's designed challenge/joy is even
reachable** — several participants describe deriving real fiero/joy from
"hard mode" access struggles once past the gate (Aaron beating nondisabled
friends despite disadvantage; Eddy streaming his access hacks for an
audience), but when the ability mismatch is too large the same struggle
just reads as exclusion, and participants abandon the game rather than
treat inaccessibility as a desirable challenge.

## Claims

- **Three-phase game adoption model** (adapted/extended from Lee et al.'s
  "discovery, access, and organization" model): **Discovery** (identify
  candidate games; general goal) / *added access goal*: identify games
  that *might* be accessible. **Evaluation** (gather info to assess
  fit) / *added access goal*: determine if a game could be "accessible
  enough." **Adaptation** (learn to play, develop playstyle, decide to
  continue or abandon) / *added access goal*: develop and implement
  solutions to access issues. Lee et al.'s original model only maps onto
  Discovery and Evaluation; **Adaptation is this paper's new third phase**,
  argued to be under-studied in HCI despite prior game-studies coverage of
  "learning"/"specialization" behaviors (Fig. 1, Section 1).
- **Access difficulty** (Section 5.2.1): building on Salen & Zimmerman's
  "artificial conflict" definition of games and on Hamilton's
  conceptualization of difficulty plus the social model of disability,
  the paper frames access difficulty as *inaccessibility understood
  through the lens of difficulty* — "a specific type of challenge present
  in a game related to the task of navigating accessibility barriers,"
  produced when a player perceives a mismatch between their abilities and
  the abilities a game was designed for. It exists **between a player and
  a game**, not as an abstract property of the game alone, and a player
  need not identify as disabled to experience it (an inexperienced gamer
  facing *Elden Ring*, or "brain fog" in a puzzle game, both count). Like
  other forms of difficulty, access difficulty "has the potential to
  produce satisfying gameplay experiences and may be actively sought out"
  — but "not all challenge... is desirable, particularly in cases when the
  ability mismatch implicit in access difficulty leaves a player feeling
  excluded."
- **Disabled gaming** (Section 5.2.2–5.2.3): the practice of engaging with
  access difficulty — identity-reading, deviant play, and queergaming
  applied to disability — characterized by *how frequently and expertly* a
  player navigates access difficulty, not by a fixed disability label.
  Disabled gamers routinely encounter access difficulty in games not
  designed with their abilities in mind; some of their "solutions" are a
  positive, identity-expressive practice, not merely deficit-repair — the
  paper explicitly warns against "siloed" accessibility solutions or
  auto-"fixing" access difficulty (its *Mario Kart* driving-assistant
  thought experiment: an auto-steer "solution" would remove Dylan and
  Mira's open-world exploration joy, Aaron's hard-mode victory, and Blue's
  access-intimacy bonding with friends — "fundamentally changes the
  experience of playing and blocks disabled gamers from being able to
  engage in unconventional, disabled gaming").
- **Consequence-based accessibility** (Section 5.3, building on Mack et
  al.): accessibility is not binary accessible/inaccessible but a personal
  calculation of expected benefit (enjoyment, health benefit) against
  potential consequence (aggravated symptoms, social discomfort). For
  participants whose disabilities could be triggered/exacerbated, there
  was a "relatively stringent threshold during Evaluation: a false-positive
  accessibility judgment... had much more severe consequences than a false
  negative." Four Adaptation behaviors sit on this calculus: **Repeated
  Discovery** (abandon and search again — last resort), **One-Time Fix**
  (single setup, treated as done), **Iterative Adaptation** (ongoing,
  evolving workaround development, itself sometimes a joy source), and
  **Adapting Expectations** (power through, or give up — "worth it" is a
  calculated risk judgment, not automatic).
- **Four Adaptation strategies** (Fig. 2, Section 4.3): **Adapting the
  Game** (in-game settings/mods — subtitles, colorblind mods, disabling
  quick-time events), **Adapting the System** (assistive tech / input-output
  reconfiguration — screen readers, adaptive controllers, remapping),
  **Adapting Expectations** (power through or abandon), **Adapting Play**
  (changing playstyle/rules/goals — e.g., choosing ranged over melee
  characters, inventing personal objectives, "world exploration" games
  chosen over competitive racing in *Mario Kart*).
- **Cross-cutting themes**: **Socially-created accessibility** (Section
  4.4.1) — friends and family co-create access (steering a ship for a
  disabled teammate, remapping controls, playing "your own game" for
  someone), producing "access intimacy" as a bonding side-effect, though
  well-intentioned peer judgments about accessibility are sometimes wrong
  and can add social pressure. **Appropriation for access** (Section
  4.4.2) — participants repurpose content never designed for accessibility
  (YouTube/Twitch gameplay footage as an accessibility preview; a
  flight-mechanic gesture-recognition mouse repurposed as a
  button-press-free input) far more than they use official/intentional
  accessibility resources.
- **Shortcomings of official accessibility resources** (Section 5.4): very
  few participants used official resources (e.g., Xbox Accessibility
  Feature Tags) despite recent industry investment — attributed to low
  awareness/visibility (resources aren't surfaced where players already
  look, e.g. store pages) and to distrust of binary "(in)accessible to
  people with [X]" judgments, since participants — as experts in their own
  disabilities — often felt official judgments didn't reflect their actual,
  highly individual access needs (echoing Mack & McDonnell et al.'s
  critique of "misrepresentation" in disability models).
- **Four design recommendations** (Section 5.5, informed throughout by
  Design Takeaways per phase): (1) **Spotlight & Develop Community
  Resources** — distributors should centralize community-created
  accessibility content (with creator credit/compensation) rather than
  requiring cross-referencing many sources. (2) **Opt-In Features &
  Customizability** — granular opt-in at input, mechanics, thematic, or
  organizational level (e.g. opting into quick-time events, gory content, a
  whole stage), explicitly *not* a request to judge who a game is
  "accessible to" — publicize options, let players decide. (3) **Social and
  Independent Access Solutions** — design for social accessibility support
  (split-role co-op, adjustable HUD/handicap options like *Battlefield*
  Hardcore Mode) *and* ensure games remain accessible without relying on
  others. (4) **Opportunities for Unconventional Play & Metagaming** — keep
  design open to non-intended playstyles rather than railroading players
  into one "correct" way to engage (citing GTA Roleplay as a model of a
  community-invented metagame going mainstream).

## Methods

- **Design**: Semi-structured, mostly one-on-one interview study
  (three main topics per Discovery/Evaluation/Adaptation), 45–75 min
  sessions, conducted mostly on Zoom; one in-person session with an
  interactive observed gameplay session (a nonverbal, non-reading
  participant paired with two family-member proxies/aides, following
  recommended practices for adjusting protocols to disabled participants'
  needs and for the role of a proxy in disability research).
- **N and recruitment**: 16 interviews conducted; **3 excluded as
  fraudulent** (participants whose screening-form answers contradicted
  their interview answers — one recognized directly by the interviewer in
  a later interview), leaving **N=13 analyzed participants**. Reported
  explicitly for transparency, "to inform researchers of the prevalence of
  fraudulent participants in online HCI and accessibility research."
  Recruited online via public posts in gamer communities and
  disability-specific spaces (e.g. Reddit's r/DisabledGamers), with
  deliberately **cross-disability, non-targeted** recruitment — criteria
  were "anyone with a disability, chronic illness, or mental health
  condition that affects how they interact with games, as well as people
  who are d/Deaf and Hard of Hearing."
- **Population** (Table 1, self-reported): 13 participants, ages 18–59
  (concentrated 18–29 and 30–39), majority Caucasian with some
  Asian/Hispanic/Latino identities, mixed gender (mostly male, one
  genderqueer participant). Disabilities spanned: autism/ADHD/developmental
  disabilities and nonverbal/non-reading (Dylan); chronic illness/chronic
  fatigue with visual-motion and exertion triggers (Blue); hemiplegia +
  tremor + speech impediment (Ken); legal blindness (Aaron); autism/ADHD/
  OCD/Auditory Processing Disorder/cPTSD with graphic-content triggers
  (Kristina); colorblindness (David); partial hemiplegia from stroke
  (Caleb); chronic illness (pain, fatigue, brain fog, audio/visual
  processing) + ADHD + cPTSD (Mira); psoriatic arthritis affecting
  dexterity, plus depression/anxiety triggers (Erich); congenital
  blindness (Avi); dexterity/manual-input condition using custom 3D-printed
  AT (Eddy); blindness + ADHD (Marc); deaf-blindness with cochlear implant
  (Tristan). Genres/platforms spanned FPS, RPG, story-driven, racing,
  adventure, and roleplay across PC, console, and mobile.
- **Analysis**: Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke), combined
  deductive-inductive coding — deductive codes from the three-phase
  framing; inductive sub-codes for "challenges," "resources," and
  "strategies" within each phase (9 sub-codes total), plus additional
  inductive codes for cross-cutting experiences. First author (who led
  coding) is neurodivergent, has 4 years professional game-accessibility
  experience and 7 years transmedia/game-hacking-for-inclusion experience;
  second and third authors (theme development) are HCI accessibility
  researchers with 10+ years' experience each and do not identify as
  disabled — positionality explicitly reported per reflexive-TA norms.
  Complete codebook released as supplementary material (not linked/coded
  in this note; treat `code_url: null` as "codebook, not software").
  NSF IIS-1702751 and UW CREATE funded.

## Results

See Claims above — the paper is organized as three Results sections
(4.1 Discovery, 4.2 Evaluation, 4.3 Adaptation) plus two cross-cutting
themes (4.4), each ending in phase-specific "Design Takeaways" that feed
directly into Section 5.5's four consolidated recommendations. No
quantitative results are reported (qualitative interview study); frequency
language ("most," "many," "some," "a few") is used descriptively, not as
counts with statistical claims.

## Critique / open questions

- **N=13, self-selected, single-country-skewed sample**: recruited from
  English-language online gaming/disability communities; the paper does
  not claim generalizability and is explicit that findings characterize
  *this* group's practices, not disabled gamers as a population. Treat as
  rich qualitative theory-building (E3), not a prevalence claim.
- **Fraudulent-participant exclusion (3/16, ~19%) is a notable methods
  finding in its own right** — worth flagging to any future study in this
  project doing paid online interview recruitment; the paper's screening
  method (cross-checking screening-form vs. interview answers) is a
  concrete mitigation worth adopting.
- **No behavioral/objective measure, no comparison group of nondisabled
  players' adoption process** — the paper's claims about *disabled gaming*
  as distinctively rich/creative rest on interview self-report and the
  authors' interpretive framing (explicitly informed by identity-based
  gaming literature — queer gaming, black gaming), not a controlled
  contrast. This is consistent with the paper's own stated aim (theory
  building, not effect measurement) but means it should anchor 8.5's
  *qualitative* mechanism, not a numeric weight.
- **"Access difficulty" and "disabled gaming" are this paper's own
  theoretical contributions**, not independently validated constructs —
  cite as E3 (grounded qualitative theory from a primary study), same tier
  as this rubric already uses for comparable single-study theoretical
  contributions (e.g. juul2013art, ballou2023just).
- **Direct tension with the rubric's existing DDA literature**: the
  paper's *Mario Kart* driving-assistant thought experiment is a strong,
  concrete illustration of why "adaptive difficulty" accessibility
  features can *remove* fun rather than add it if they collapse the
  challenge a player actually wants — this sharpens (does not contradict)
  8.5's existing caution that DDA's pooled enjoyment effect is null; it
  supplies the mechanism (a poorly-targeted "accessibility" fix can delete
  the exact challenge-type a player was seeking) for *why* some DDA
  implementations might net negative even when framed as inclusive.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 5** — This is the strongest available primary source for
  Dimension 8.5 (Accessibility of difficulty/controls), which the rubric
  currently backs only with caroux2023player (DDA meta-analysis, general
  difficulty not accessibility) and hicks2018good/sweetser2012revisiting
  (general feedback taxonomies, not accessibility-specific). It directly
  seeds a new concept (`accessibility-as-gate-on-joy`) that reframes 8.5
  from a pure "subtractor/hygiene" reading toward an explicit **gate**

## Rubric implications

- **8.5 Accessibility of difficulty/controls — SUPPORTS and reframes,
  esp. this criterion** (docs/rubric.md v0.4). The current anchor language
  ("options broaden who reaches flow... redundant multimodal feedback
  doubles as accessibility") is a *feature-checklist* framing. This paper
  supplies the *mechanism* underneath it: accessibility barriers are a
  **gate** on whether a player can reach the game's designed
  challenge/joy at all — not a subtractor that merely lowers a score
  alongside other functional criteria, but a precondition without which
  no other dimension's score is reachable for that player. Concretely:
  Aaron (legally blind) describes real fiero from beating nondisabled
  friends *despite* disadvantage in *Battlefield*; Eddy streams his access
  hacks as a point of pride and audience engagement; but Caleb simply could
  not clear a wall-jump sequence needing more simultaneous inputs than he
  could manage, and gave up — same "hard mode" framing, opposite outcome,
  because the ability mismatch was too large to cross the gate at all.
  Recommend: (a) add martinez2024playing as a primary 8.5 source
  alongside caroux2023player; (b) consider revising 8.5's 0/2/4 anchors to
  explicitly distinguish "no accessibility options — some players cannot
  reach the core loop at all" (0) from "options exist but require
  cross-referencing outside resources / mods / social help to discover"
  (2) from "in-game, opt-in, granular options surfaced where players
  already look (store page, settings), letting the player themselves
  calculate the consequence-based trade-off" (4) — this maps directly onto
  the paper's Discovery/Evaluation/Adaptation phases and its "official
  resource" critique (Section 5.4).
- **G1/G2 hard gates — analogous structure, worth a cross-reference**.
  The rubric's existing gate language ("a 0 on either gate caps the game
  at 'not fun' regardless of the rest") is structurally the same claim
  this paper makes about access difficulty: below some ability-mismatch
  threshold, no amount of designed challenge or juice matters because the
  player cannot engage with the loop at all. Worth a one-line
  cross-reference from 8.5 to the Hard Gates section noting that for a
  disabled player, an unaddressed access barrier *functions as a
  third, player-relative gate* even though the rubric does not (and
  should not) add a universal G3 — accessibility is evaluated relative to
  a specific player's abilities, not a universal pass/fail like G1/G2.
- **3.1 Difficulty tracks skill / DDA caution — nuances**. Sharpens the
  existing DDA caution (caroux2023player's null pooled effect;
  denisova2015adaptation's small positive on immersion) with a concrete
  failure mode: the *Mario Kart* driving-assistant thought experiment
  shows a well-intentioned adaptive-difficulty "accessibility" feature can
  net-negative fun by deleting the specific challenge type (spatial/motor
  precision) the player was there for, rather than recalibrating it. This
  is a design-theory-level (E4-adjacent, grounded in the E3 interview
  data) explanation for *why* DDA's pooled effect might wash out — some
  implementations help, some actively remove the thing being measured.
- **2.4 Multiple valid approaches / 6.5 Discovery is player-authored —
  supports**. The paper's "Adapting Play" strategies (choosing ranged over
  melee, inventing personal objectives, playing *GTA V* purely for
  roleplay) and its explicit design recommendation to support
  "Unconventional Play & Metagaming" are direct qualitative evidence for
  these two criteria's top anchors ("approaches the designer didn't
  anticipate work" / "optional depth for those who look") — cite
  martinez2024playing alongside deterding2015lens and sweetser2005gameflow
  Control for genre-agnostic support, not just as accessibility-specific.
- **No support found for**: any numeric weight for 8.5 relative to other
  Dimension 8 criteria, or for the rubric's overall Dimension 8 weight
  (5%) — this paper doesn't address cross-dimension weighting, only
  within-8.5 mechanism and design recommendations.
