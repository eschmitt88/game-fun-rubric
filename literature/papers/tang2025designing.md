# Designing for Curiosity: Understanding Player Engagement with Treasure Chest Mechanics in Open-World Games

## TL;DR

A qualitative, peer-reviewed study (semi-structured interviews, N=19
experienced Assassin's Creed players, 50-500 hrs each, Reflexive Thematic
Analysis) that treats treasure chests — not reward systems in general — as
the unit of design analysis, and reads player engagement with them through
a six-type curiosity classification (Tang & Kirman, 2024): Perceptual (PC),
Manipulatory (MC), Curiosity about Complexity/Ambiguity (CCA), Epistemic
(EC), Social (SC), and a novel **Future Rewards Maximization Curiosity
(FRMC)**. Four themes emerged: (1) Multidimensional Motivational Drivers,
(2) Player-Chest Dynamic Interactions (mutual influence / strategy
optimization), (3) Chests as Game-Progression Catalysts, and (4) Emotional
Resonance — achievement, control, belonging, and **fatigue**. Headline
finding: PC (visual/audio appeal) is a one-time, short-lived trigger that
converts into CCA within the first 3-5 chest openings as players decode the
reward pool; sustained engagement then runs on FRMC (strategic
reward-maximizing) and EC (decoding the reward *logic*), not on the initial
curiosity that got them to open the first chest. Excess chest density or
predictable/disproportionate rewards produces a **discovery-fatigue**
effect explained via diminishing marginal utility and habituation.

## Claims

- **Curiosity-driven vs. utility-driven engagement are conceptually
  distinct** but interacting processes (Section 3, framing move before the
  four themes): curiosity-driven = intrinsic attraction to chests as
  objects of interest (opening, discovering, anticipating); utility-driven
  = instrumental value of chest *contents* for progression/customization.
  Players can be curious about low-utility chests and can seek high-utility
  contents with little curiosity — the paper tracks both threads throughout.
- **PC (perceptual curiosity) is the near-universal *first* trigger but is
  short-lived and largely a "visual anchor" after the first encounter** —
  once chest contents stop changing, appearance alone stops sustaining
  interest (Section 3.1.4, Section 4.1): "the impact of perceptual curiosity
  seems relatively short-lived, with players more concerned about the
  diversity and value of chest contents than their appearance and sound."
- **CCA saturates fast and predictably**: most participants reported they
  could roughly decode a chest's reward-pool pattern within **3-5
  openings** ("More than ten times, I can't stand it" — P8), regardless of
  self-reported game experience level — a concrete, falsifiable dose figure
  the rubric's existing sources don't supply (Section 3.1.1).
- **Content updates can restart a decayed CCA cycle** ("they added a new
  treasure chest today, what kind of things might be inside?" — P11), but
  the paper explicitly flags the durability of this renewed interest as
  untested (Section 3.1.1) — a stated open question, not a claim.
- **FRMC (Future Rewards Maximization Curiosity) is proposed as a genuinely
  new curiosity type**, distinct from CCA: CCA wants to *experience* the
  unknown; FRMC wants to *maximize* known-valuable outcomes, and is
  "driven and optimized by experience" — so it is uniquely vulnerable to
  **expectation violation**: when high-difficulty acquisition doesn't pay
  off in high-value reward, FRMC (not CCA) collapses first (Section 3.1.3,
  quoting P11: "challenging high-level monsters at a low level doesn't seem
  to give any reward bonus. That's quite uninteresting").
- **Predictability/surprise balance, not maximal unpredictability, sustains
  engagement** (Section 3.2.2, "Balance of Transparency"): players want
  enough transparency to plan around specific needed items ("I specifically
  need a certain item, so I need to know it's inside to open it
  accordingly" — P6) alongside enough surprise to avoid "monotonous or
  boring" (P13); one participant's own informal ratio estimate (60-70%
  surprise / 30-40% predictability) is flagged by the authors as **one
  individual's opinion, not a consensus finding or a validated instrument**.
- **Strategy evolves in a specific, reported direction with playtime**:
  exhaustive early-game "open everything" exploration gives way to
  selective, value-calibrated late-game chest-opening, driven by FRMC+EC
  jointly (Sections 3.2.1, 4.1) — players begin bypassing "seemingly
  tempting chests at crucial moments" (P14) and route around low-yield
  areas via community-sourced information (P19) once they've built a
  personal reward-pattern model.
- **Discovery fatigue is a named, load-bearing finding** (Section 3.4.4,
  "Sense of Fatigue"; General Discussion 4.4; Design Recommendations
  7.1.4): excessive chest exposure and dense chest distribution over short
  time periods produce diminishing engagement, independent of and additive
  to the reward-difficulty mismatch above. The paper explains it via two
  external mechanisms it imports rather than tests directly — **diminishing
  marginal utility** (Etemadi et al., 2023, behavioral economics) and the
  **scarcity principle** (Kovalenkov & Wooders, 1999) plus **habituation
  theory** (Hall & Rodríguez, 2020) — i.e. reported player fatigue is
  interpreted through these frameworks post hoc, not independently measured
  against them.
- **Sense of control is not a demand for predictability** but a demand for
  *consistent internal logic*: players tolerate chests they can't fully
  predict but reject chests whose difficulty-to-reward relationship
  contradicts the game's own established rules (P11's "obviously more
  difficult... doesn't yield particularly valuable content" complaint;
  contrast with the *Dark Souls* monster-chest example, P14, which is
  accepted by fans specifically because it is a *consistent* rule of that
  game, not because it is predictable) (Section 3.4.2).
- **Chests carry a social-curiosity / attachment component in nominally
  single-player play** (Section 3.4.3, Sense of Belonging): historically-
  grounded chest placement and lore fragments deepen "sense of reality" and
  world-attachment (P7, P16); some players report emotional attachment to
  chest-sourced gear they no longer use (P12) and treat cumulative
  chest-opening stats as "fond remembrance" (P3) rather than pure
  achievement — the authors read this as SC (social curiosity, per Tang &
  Kirman 2024's definition extending to role/identity exploration) but
  explicitly flag the role-immersion↔SC link as **under-theorized, not
  established** (Section 3.4.1).
- **Player-explored chests (the paper's studied object) are structurally
  distinct from system-awarded chests**: the former require active,
  forgoable player exploration; the latter are simply handed to the player
  with no opt-out (Section 1.1). The paper's whole design-recommendation
  set targets the player-explored category specifically, deliberately
  excluding monetized/loot-box mechanics (Section 5, Limitations).
- **Six-type, not five- or seven-type, curiosity taxonomy used here**: PC,
  MC, CCA, EC, SC (all from Tang & Kirman 2024, itself building on
  Kreitler et al. 1975 and Loewenstein 1994) **plus the newly-proposed
  FRMC** — note this differs in composition from both to2016integrating's
  five Kreitler-derived types and kumari2019role's seven uncertainty types;
  the three frameworks are not a strict superset/subset of one another and
  cross-mapping them is left to future work, not attempted in this paper.

## Methods

- **Design**: qualitative, Reflexive Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke
  2019/2021, six-step method per Byrne 2022); researcher positionality
  disclosed (both authors are fans of the AC chest experience; mitigated
  by reflective diaries during a self-conducted *Assassin's Creed Valhalla*
  playthrough, not eliminated) (Section 2.5).
- **Sample**: 19 of 68 initial respondents (25 met eligibility), recruited
  via Reddit, university email, researcher contacts, and offline posters.
  Inclusion: age ≥18, 50-500 hrs of AC playtime (mean 187h, SD 112.3; the
  50-500h band = mean ±1 SD of Steam platform playtime data, an empirical
  but not preregistered cutoff), played AC within the past week. 13 of 19
  had played multiple AC titles. 30-60 min semi-structured video interviews
  (mean 47 min), £15 Amazon-voucher compensation, university ethics
  approval (Section 2.1-2.4).
- **Saturation**: reached at ~10 interviews by the authors' own account,
  continued to 19 for demographic diversity and unresolved sub-questions
  (transparency levels, emotional responses); stopping rule = no
  substantially new themes across 3 consecutive interviews + comprehensive
  perspective coverage + thematic depth, not a fixed N (Section 2.4.1) —
  methodologically consistent with kumari2019role's saturation-based N=13.
- **Case-study scope, explicit and narrow by design**: single franchise
  (Assassin's Creed, 14 titles, 2007-2025, ~€4bn revenue, >200M copies) as
  the sole empirical context; explicitly critiqued in-paper as
  comparatively *un*-innovative in curiosity-driven exploration relative to
  *Breath of the Wild* or *Skyrim* (Section 1.4, citing Kohler 2017, Bogost
  2016) — chosen for the density/diversity of its chest implementations and
  community salience, not as an exemplar of best-in-class chest design.
  Definitionally scoped to **traditional container-representation chests
  only** (wooden boxes, coffers) — excludes functionally-similar objects
  (Persona's glowing points, Pokémon's Pokéballs) even though the paper
  cites to2016integrating's finding that these may trigger comparable
  psychological responses (Section 1.1).

## Results

Purely qualitative — no counts, effect sizes, or inferential statistics
anywhere in the paper; every claim is supported by RTA-coded interview
excerpts, cross-participant pattern description ("15/19 emphasized X"-style
counts appear a few times as descriptive tallies, not as inferential
evidence), and post-hoc alignment to external theory (Loewenstein,
Berlyne, Kashdan et al. 2009's multidimensional curiosity model,
Csikszentmihalyi flow, diminishing-marginal-utility/scarcity/habituation
literatures). The four-theme, twelve-sub-theme structure (Table 1, not
extracted here) and the mapping of each sub-theme onto one or more of the
six curiosity types are the paper's primary results artifacts, directly
answering its own three research questions (RQ1-RQ3, Section 6, verbatim
answers given).

## Critique / open questions

- **N=19, single-franchise, single-genre qualitative study — the authors
  are explicit about all three limits** (Section 5): findings are not
  claimed to generalize beyond experienced AC-style open-world players;
  casual players and other genres are out of scope; the commercial-series
  choice sacrifices experimental control over design variables entirely
  (no manipulated chest-design conditions — every finding is about
  *existing*, non-experimentally-varied AC chest design, read through
  player report).
- **No quantitative threshold for "too dense" is established** despite
  fatigue being a headline finding — the paper explicitly names this as
  future work (Section 5, Section 7.2.2: "determine specific thresholds
  for chest frequency that trigger disengagement" is listed as an open
  research direction, not something this paper answers).
- **The diminishing-marginal-utility / scarcity-principle / habituation
  explanations for fatigue are imported from behavioral economics and
  psychology, not independently tested against this dataset** — they are
  offered as the most plausible existing mechanism, not verified via, e.g.,
  a dose-response design.
- **Recency/experience selection criteria are a double-edged design choice
  the authors themselves flag**: restricting to 50-500h players "cannot
  capture the initial curiosity experienced by new players encountering
  chests for the first time" (Section 2.1) — directly limiting the paper's
  ability to speak to first-session curiosity, which is exactly the PC-
  driven "front-loaded" onboarding phase that rubric criterion 6.1 also
  has to account for.
- **Participant self-report of a numeric surprise/predictability ratio
  (P10's 60-70/30-40 split) is a single anecdote elevated to illustrative
  status in the abstract-adjacent discussion** — worth citing as an
  illustration of the *shape* of the preference (surprise-favoring, not
  50/50), not as a validated ratio.
- **Reflexivity is disclosed but not eliminated**: both researchers'
  self-described fondness for AC chests and pre-existing theoretical
  commitment to curiosity-as-explanatory-frame is a standard RTA
  limitation, handled via the same kind of self-monitoring
  kumari2019role/deterding2015joys use, not resolved by independent coding.
- **Strong complementary fit, not redundancy, with the existing evidence
  base**: this paper independently arrives at the same
  predictability-vs-surprise balance point kumari2019role's Result
  Uncertainty inverted-U describes (5.2) and the same
  gap-size-vs-confidence-to-close-it distinction to2016integrating draws
  from Loewenstein (6.3) — but does so via a different game genre
  (single-player open-world action-RPG vs. kumari2019role's casual mobile)
  and a narrower design object (one mechanic: chests, vs. m2m motivation
  generally), which is exactly the kind of converging-methods evidence the
  rubric's evidence-tier discipline treats as strengthening rather than
  merely repeating a claim.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 4** — Directly strengthens dimension 6 (currently
  E1/E2/E3/E4 mixed-tier), specifically: adds E3 qualitative evidence and
  a concrete failure-mode name ("discovery fatigue") to 6.1's rate-of-
  content anchors (currently E4-designer-theory-only); and gives 6.5's
  "discovery is player-authored" criterion its clearest empirical
  grounding yet via the paper's player-explored vs. system-awarded chest
  distinction. Also corroborates 5.2 and 6.3's existing predictability/
  surprise and information-gap framing from an independent game genre and
  method-scope. Short of a 5 because it's single-franchise qualitative
  evidence extending, not founding, criteria that already have some E1-E3
  backing.
- Consider fetching **Tang & Kirman (2024)**, the source of the six-type
  curiosity classification (PC/MC/CCA/EC/SC/FRMC) this paper builds on —
  likely load-bearing for dimension 6 if it turns out to be a standalone,
  independently citable framework paper rather than a workshop note.
- Consider fetching **Kashdan et al. (2009)**'s multidimensional curiosity
  model, cited here (Section 4.1) as convergent with the six-type finding —
  a possible independent-of-games anchor for dimension 6's psychology base,
  parallel to how Loewenstein (via to2016integrating) already anchors 6.3.
- The paper's own explicitly flagged future-work list (Section 7.2) —
  quantitative chest-frequency thresholds, cross-genre replication,
  monetization/loot-box curiosity interactions — maps directly onto open
  gaps this project's evidence-synthesis doc (`docs/analysis/`) should
  track for dimension 6 and 5.2.

## Rubric implications

- **6.1 (rate of new content/mechanics across the runtime) — new
  supporting evidence, currently E4-only.** This paper's discovery-fatigue
  finding (Sections 3.4.4, 4.4, 7.1.4) is close to a direct empirical
  referent for 6.1's "front-loaded then flat" (0-anchor) vs. "surprises
  through to the end" (4-anchor) spectrum: PC/CCA measurably decay within
  ~3-5 exposures to a static reward pool, and dense/dis-proportionate
  chest frequency independently produces fatigue via diminishing marginal
  utility and habituation. **Propose adding `tang2025designing` to 6.1's
  citekeys** and upgrading the tier note from pure E4 to "E4 designer
  theory, E3 qualitative corroboration + specific decay dose (3-5
  exposures) and named failure mode (discovery fatigue)."
- **6.5 (discovery is player-authored) — sharper empirical grounding,
  currently E4-only (deterding2015lens).** The paper's structural
  distinction between **player-explored chests** (active, forgoable
  discovery — the paper's entire object of study) and **system-awarded
  chests** (handed to the player, no opt-out) (Section 1.1) is a clean,
  independently-derived operationalization of exactly what 6.5's 0-vs-4
  anchor ("all signposted" vs. "optional depth for those who look") is
  trying to capture. **Propose adding `tang2025designing` alongside
  `deterding2015lens` for 6.5**, noting the tier moves toward "E4 lens +
  E3 qualitative operationalization from a large commercial case study."
- **6.3 (information gaps) — corroborates, doesn't newly anchor.** The
  Balance-of-Transparency finding (3.2.2) independently replicates the
  same predictability/surprise-tension shape 6.3 already cites via
  malone1981toward/hicks2019juicy/to2016integrating/kumari2019role, from a
  different game (AC) and a narrower mechanic (chests specifically). Worth
  a citation addition for triangulation; **no tier change** — this
  strengthens an already E1-E3-anchored criterion rather than raising it.
- **5.2 (uncertain outcome) — minor corroboration.** FRMC's
  expectation-violation collapse (difficulty not matching reward breaks
  engagement, Section 3.1.3) is consistent with, not novel beyond,
  kumari2019role's Result Uncertainty inverted-U already cited at E3 here.
  Optional citation add, no tier change.
- **New concept candidate: "discovery fatigue" / reward-density
  calibration.** Neither `reward-schedule-taxonomy` nor
  `information-gap-curiosity` currently names the specific failure mode of
  *too much* curiosity-triggering content too densely packed (as distinct
  from too little). **Propose extending `reward-schedule-taxonomy.md`'s
  `sources:` to include `tang2025designing`** (it already anchors
  hopson2001behavioral's reinforcement-schedule vocabulary; this paper adds
  the diminishing-marginal-utility/habituation mechanism for *why* dense
  variable-ratio-like reward schedules specifically fatigue rather than
  merely satiate) rather than proposing a wholly new concept file — the
  mechanism is a refinement of an existing concept, not a new one.
- **No support for reweighting.** Nothing here bears on dimension 6's 10%
  weight or any other dimension's weight — consistent with
  `docs/rubric.md`'s standing caution that weights stay provisional
  pending studies varying design factors against retention/session-length
  directly (this paper explicitly defers that exact question to future
  work, Section 7.2.2).
- **Contradicts nothing** in the current rubric; additive and
  corroborating throughout, with one small nuance worth carrying into
  6.5's prose: "player-authored discovery" should not be read as "no
  signposting at all" — the paper's Sense-of-Control finding (3.4.2) shows
  players reject *inconsistent* internal logic even when they explicitly
  don't want full predictability, i.e. the 4-anchor ("optional depth for
  those who look") needs the game's underlying reward rules to still be
  *fair and legible in retrospect*, not merely hidden.
