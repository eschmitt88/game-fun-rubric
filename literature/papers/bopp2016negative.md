## TL;DR

An online survey (N=121 players) asked participants to recount one
*emotionally moving* game experience (not restricted to "fun") and rated it
on validated enjoyment, appreciation, affect (happy/sad/meaningful), and
need-satisfaction (PENS + Bartsch's media "emotional gratification" scales)
measures, plus open-ended qualitative coding of causes. Sadness was the
single most-mentioned emotion (n=58/121), yet both enjoyment (M=5.95/7) and
appreciation (M=5.69/7) scored high, and sad affect *positively* predicted
both enjoyment (β=.32, p<.01) and appreciation (β=.45, p<.01). The paper's
core claim: negatively-valenced emotion in games is not merely tolerated
alongside fun — it can independently drive a distinct positive-experience
construct ("appreciation": feeling moved, thought-provoked) that fun-only
enjoyment measures miss entirely.

## Claims

- **Core finding** (Abstract; Discussion p.26): "players did value their
  experience *not in spite* of negative emotions, but actually *thanks* to
  the game inspiring strong emotional reactions, including sadness... not
  only positive emotions are fun, but negative affect may also contribute
  to a positive player experience."
- **Enjoyment and appreciation are dissociable constructs with different
  predictors** (Results, Table 2, p.17): regression β weights — Enjoyment
  is predicted by happy affect (β=.51**), sad affect (β=.32**), autonomy
  (β=.25*), character engagement (β=.29*), competence (β=.22*), experiencing
  emotions (β=.21*). Appreciation is predicted by sad affect (β=.45**),
  contemplativeness (β=.46**), meaningful affect (β=.40**), experiencing
  emotions (β=.29*) — and *not* by competence or autonomy. Full models:
  needs R²adj=.42 (enjoyment) / .45 (appreciation); affect R²adj=.33
  (enjoyment) / .51 (appreciation), all p<.001.
- **Emotion catalogue from open-response coding** (Results, pp.17-18,
  N=121, sentence-level qualitative coding, multi-code allowed): sadness/
  grief/heartbreak n=58; shock/fear/anxiety n=21; frustration/anger n=19;
  guilt/regret n=5; fun/joy/happiness n=17; achievement/pride/satisfaction
  n=9; awe/amazement n=8; mixed positive-negative affect explicitly named
  n=29; intense-but-unspecified-valence n=39.
- **Five game/player components that caused emotionally moving experiences**
  (qualitative themes, coded from N=121 accounts, inter-rater κ=.787 over 80
  double-coded cases, range .637-1.000): (1) **Loss** — in-game character
  death/separation, n=63 (>half the sample); high sadness (M=6.14/7) *and*
  high enjoyment (M=6.07) and appreciation (M=5.97) simultaneously. (2)
  **Character attachment**, n=85 (most common single theme) — bond formed
  through time invested and getting to know a character; reflected in high
  character-engagement scores (M=5.40, SD=1.54). (3) **Agency and
  responsibility** — decisions forced players to own consequences; decisions
  (forced or not) raised autonomy scores (M=5.58, SD=0.97) above the sample
  mean (M=4.69, SD=1.46), but responsibility for outcomes (e.g., a
  companion's death resulting from the player's choice) specifically evoked
  guilt. Lack of agency, conversely, evoked anger/helplessness. (4)
  **Achievement** — the only theme dominated by purely positive affect
  (lowest sad-affect score of all themes, M=3.64, SD=1.59). (5)
  **Atmosphere** — audiovisual immersion alone (music, ambient sound,
  visuals), n=29, evoking awe/amazement or fear with no narrative loss
  event required.
- **Personal memories and self-reflection were unprompted but pervasive**
  (Results p.24-25): players spontaneously connected in-game loss to real
  personal losses (bereavement) and reported behavior change ("Now I try to
  be more helpful to others," P54). Not solicited by the survey questions —
  emerged from open coding. Nearly half (49.6%) of recalled experiences had
  occurred more than two years prior, offered as evidence the experiences
  "linger."
- **Reliability of instruments** (Table 1, p.14): all Cronbach's α ≥ .68
  (autonomy weakest at .68; character-engagement/contemplativeness/social-
  sharing all ≥.89). Enjoyment and appreciation scales are Oliver & Bartsch
  (2010), validated originally on film, applied here to games (following
  prior game-PX use by Oliver et al. 2015 and Steinemann et al. 2015).

## Methods

- Cross-sectional online survey, N=121 (of 127 completed; 6 excluded for
  not recounting a single game experience), recruited via gaming forums
  (Talk Nintendo, TV Tropes) and Facebook gaming groups/social networks.
  23.1% female; age 14-48 (M=26.27); avg. 17.47 years gaming experience.
  Lottery incentive (12× $10 Steam gift cards).
  Preceded by a 12-participant semi-structured interview pilot (thematic
  analysis, Braun & Clarke 2006) to validate that Bartsch's (2012) film
  "emotional gratifications" taxonomy transfers to games — it broadly did,
  plus players added an "achievement" gratification the film taxonomy
  lacks.
  Open prompt: "bring to mind an emotionally moving experience... describe
  as accurately and detailed as you remember in at least 50 words," followed
  by a clarifying prompt on causes/thoughts/feelings.
  Quantitative measures (all 7-point Likert): Enjoyment & Appreciation
  (Oliver & Bartsch 2010); Happy/Sad/Meaningful affect (Oliver & Raney
  2011); PENS autonomy/competence (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006) —
  relatedness dropped after factor analysis showed it loaded onto the same
  factor as "social sharing"; and Bartsch's (2012) six media emotional-
  gratification subscales (character engagement, contemplativeness,
  experiencing emotions/"empathic sadness" adapted, social sharing,
  vicarious release), one item modified for games.
  Qualitative coding: expanded version of Gabrielsson & Lindström-Wik's
  (2003) "strong experiences with music" descriptive coding scheme, grown
  from 30 seed transcripts to 14 main categories / 42 subcategories;
  sentence-level unit of analysis, multi-label; single coder (first
  author) with 80/121 accounts double-coded for reliability (mean κ=.787,
  range .637-1.000).
  Analysis: hierarchical multiple regression ("Enter" method), controlling
  age/gender, separately for the 7-predictor needs/gratifications model and
  the 3-predictor affect model, against enjoyment and appreciation as DVs
  (4 regressions total); partial correlations between needs and affect.

## Critique / open questions

- **Explicitly exploratory/correlational, no causal claim** (Limitations,
  p.32, the authors' own words): "no causal inferences may be made about
  what factors actually caused players' emotional reactions." The five
  qualitative themes are researcher-coded categories from retrospective
  self-report, not experimentally manipulated conditions.
- **Retrospective recall bias, acknowledged by the authors**: nearly half
  the sample recalled events >2 years old; the authors themselves flag
  (citing Kahneman & Riis 2005) that retrospective evaluation skews toward
  peak-moment intensity rather than the actual in-the-moment experience —
  weakens the "lingers" claim as evidence of durable positive PX (it may
  equally be peak-end recall bias) rather than confirming it.
  the "lingers" framing needs it flagged as a plausible confound, not
  disproof; genuine limitation either way.
- **Self-selection**: recruited from gaming forums/Facebook gaming groups
  specifically for an "emotionally moving" experience prompt — likely
  over-samples players who both play emotionally rich genres (RPG/action-
  RPG dominate at n=109/121) and are receptive to game-driven emotion,
  which the authors concede limits generalization to players who came in
  expecting purely "fun" gameplay (p.32, citing Iacovides & Cox 2015).
- **Correlational β's confound design intent and player disposition**: a
  β=.32 for sad affect predicting enjoyment says players who felt sadder
  also reported more enjoyment — it doesn't establish that *designing for*
  sadness reliably produces enjoyment for a given target audience; individual
  differences (e.g., trait empathy, prior loss experience — several
  participants explicitly link the emotional reaction to real
  bereavement) are uncontrolled.
- **Single coder, no pre-registration**: primary qualitative coding was by
  one author; reliability check (κ) covers agreement on an *existing*
  coding scheme applied to a subset, not independent scheme derivation —
  standard practice but worth noting given how much of the paper's
  narrative rests on the five-theme structure.
- Distinguishing empirical from designer-opinion content: the quantitative
  results (regression β's, group means, category frequencies, κ) are firm
  empirical findings (E2 by the rubric's own tier definition — large-N
  correlational study with validated psychometric instruments). The
  Discussion's design recommendations ("deliberately restricting player
  autonomy can make for an intensely emotional experience... when players
  care for characters") are the authors' interpretive extrapolation, closer
  to E4 designer-theory framing than to a tested claim.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 4** — Does not anchor an existing rubric criterion the way
  e.g. juul2013art anchors 1.3/3.3, but it materially strengthens Dimension
  7 (Emotion, fantasy & narrative), which currently has thin empirical
  backing for its "emotional range" and "meaning/afterglow" criteria (7.2,
  7.5 — see Rubric implications). It also surfaces a construct (appreciation
  as distinct from enjoyment) that the rubric doesn't currently
  operationalize anywhere, which is why it doesn't clear relevance 5 despite
  strong evidence quality — it extends rather than anchors.
- Read the CHI '16 published version directly if institutional ACM access
  becomes available, to confirm no results changed between thesis and
  publication (unlikely given exact metadata match, but unverified).
- Follow the paper's own flagged follow-up: Mekler & Bopp (2015, cited on
  p.32) on applying "meta-emotion" (emotional reaction to one's own
  emotion) to games — may be a useful companion source for Dimension 7.

## Rubric implications

- **7.2 Emotional range** (Lazzaro's palette anchor: "fear/dread, relief,
  wonder/awe, amusement, fiero/triumph") — **SUPPORTS and extends**. The
  paper's empirically-coded emotion catalogue (sadness, shock/fear, anger/
  frustration, guilt, fun/joy, pride, awe, explicit mixed-affect) is a
  broader, empirically-grounded palette than Lazzaro's armchair list and
  should be cited alongside lazzaro2004why as evidence for this criterion's
  anchor, not just design theory. Currently tagged E3; this source is E2
  (large-N, validated instruments) and directly on-topic — recommend adding
  bopp2016negative as a co-source and reconsidering the tier upward for 7.2
  specifically (the *range* claim, not the *cause* claims).
- **7.5 Meaning/afterglow** — **SUPPORTS with E2 evidence**, addressing the
  rubric's own noted gap ("PXI Meaning measures in-play, not afterglow").
  Contemplativeness (β=.46**) and meaningful affect (β=.40**) predicted
  *appreciation* specifically (not enjoyment), and appreciation is
  explicitly the "thought-provoking, lingers" construct (Oliver & Bartsch
  2010). ~49.6% of recalled experiences were >2 years old, offered as (weak,
  author-flagged) afterglow evidence. Recommend citing bopp2016negative as
  the primary source actually measuring afterglow/appreciation as distinct
  from in-play enjoyment, which no current 7.5 source does.
- **Proposed new criterion consideration for Dimension 7**: the rubric
  explicitly credits designed *positive*-valence emotional peaks (5.5
  Fiero moments) but Dimension 7 has no equivalent explicit anchor for
  designed *negative*-valence peaks (loss, grief, guilt) as a legitimate
  design target in their own right — 7.2's "palette" language gestures at
  it but the 0/2/4 anchors don't distinguish "some negative emotion
  present" from "negative emotion deliberately engineered and lands." This
  paper's finding that Loss (n=63, >half the sample) was simultaneously the
  saddest theme (M=6.14 sad affect) *and* rated among the highest on both
  enjoyment (M=6.07) and appreciation (M=5.97) is direct E2 support for
  adding an explicit "designed sorrow/loss peak" anchor to 7.2's top band,
  parallel to 5.5's fiero — justification: without it, a designer optimizing
  naively against the 0-4 anchors as written could read "emotional range"
  as satisfied by variety alone and miss that *negative* peaks specifically
  need deliberate design attention (character investment time, narrative
  stakes) the same way fiero does.
- **2.2 Trade-offs, not puzzles / 2.3 Consequences persist and are
  legible** — **nuances, doesn't contradict**. The paper's "Agency and
  responsibility" theme shows morally-weighted decisions (not simple
  goods-vs-goods trade-offs) driving both elevated autonomy scores (M=5.58
  vs M=4.69 baseline) and guilt when consequences were negative — e.g.
  players "evaluate every decision extremely cautious" once they know a
  companion could die. 2.2's anchor language ("choices are between goods,
  not right/wrong") describes strategic trade-offs; this evidence is about
  a different, also-valuable decision type — ethically fraught,
  consequence-legible choices — that 2.3 already partly covers
  ("consequences shape the run") but could cite more directly. No
  criterion change recommended, but bopp2016negative is good supporting
  evidence for 2.3's top anchor specifically.
- **3.2 Failure cost is calibrated / 8.4 Setback punishment is minimal** —
  **flags a scope boundary, not a contradiction**. Both criteria are framed
  around mechanical failure (retry cost, replay burden). This paper's Loss
  theme shows *narratively permanent, mechanically irreversible* loss
  (a character's death) rated highly enjoyable/appreciated despite zero
  "retry" — because the cost is emotional/narrative, not a mechanical
  setback the player must redo. Worth a one-line editorial note under 3.2
  or 8.4 clarifying that permanent narrative loss is scored under Dimension
  7, not penalized under 3.2/8.4's failure-cost anchors, to prevent a rater
  from mis-scoring a deliberately permanent story beat as a 3.2 failure.
- **No support found for**: any specific numeric weight for Dimension 7
  (10%) — the paper doesn't address cross-dimension weighting, only
  within-dimension emotional-range/appreciation mechanics.
