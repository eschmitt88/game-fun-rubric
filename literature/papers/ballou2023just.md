# 'I Just Wanted to Get It Over and Done With': A Grounded Theory of Psychological Need Frustration in Video Games

## TL;DR

Semi-structured interviews with 12 players (grounded theory, MaxQDA-coded)
produce a four-component causal model: players hold (largely unconscious)
**expectations** for autonomy/competence/relatedness fulfilment; **felt need
frustration** arises not from a negative event per se but from the **delta**
between an expected and an observed need-thwarting situation; repeated
salient frustration updates expectations and drives **behavior change**
(reduce intensity → adapt play → disengage → quit); and **extrinsic
motivation** (curiosity about the game beyond the obstacle, fan identity,
rewards/achievements) can override this and sustain play despite frustration.
Need frustration is empirically distinct from the mere absence of need
satisfaction, has nine specific game-derived subcategories not captured by
existing SDT questionnaires, and behaves like a predictive-processing /
expectancy-violation phenomenon rather than a simple stimulus-response one.

## Claims

- Need frustration (autonomy, competence, relatedness) is "a common and
  impactful experience in games, with distinct antecedents not fully
  captured in existing measures" and is empirically separable from the
  absence of need satisfaction, not merely its inverse (Abstract, p.1;
  citing Allen & Anderson 2018, Vansteenkiste et al. 2020 for the general
  distinction, corroborated qualitatively here for games specifically).
- **Nine need-frustrating situation types**, three per need (p.8–10):
  - *Autonomy*: desired playstyle constrained (most common — e.g. forced
    non-preferred hero/role, unskippable non-preferred content);
    ability to play constrained (cost, hardware, schedule); compelled to
    play (social pressure / penalty-for-leaving mechanics in multiplayer).
  - *Competence*: stagnation (progress expected but blocked — "I can play
    this game, I know how to play it... why can't I do it now"); unfair
    situations (AI/opponent advantage perceived as illegitimate — blame is
    directed *outward*, at the game or opponent, not inward at the self);
    meaningless actions/choices (illusory agency — the *Outer Worlds*
    example: branching dialogue that converges to the same outcome
    regardless of choice, p.9).
  - *Relatedness*: disconnected from other players (harassment, skill
    mismatch, exclusion); disconnected from the community (in-group/
    out-group cliquishness even within a game one is otherwise skilled at);
    disconnected from the game's world/characters (setting or tone the
    player doesn't want to be in).
- **Core mechanism (Figure 1, p.8)**: felt frustration occurs when a
  need-thwarting situation is "more salient or intense than expected" —
  appraisal compares actual to *expected* need frustration/satisfaction, not
  actual against some absolute or ideal state (p.10–11, 14). This is framed
  explicitly against Burgoon's expectancy violation theory and Clark's
  predictive-processing account of cognition (p.14–15): motivation tracks
  *expected error reduction*, and affect follows the delta between expected
  and actual progress, not the raw valence of the event.
- Expectation shifts operate on a **session/session-to-session timescale**,
  not moment-to-moment: occasional segment-level frustration is itself
  expected and tolerated without behavior change; only *repeated or
  sustained* violation updates the more global expectation that then
  changes play behavior (p.15). One quoted mechanism: an overpowered
  character stops being just annoying and crosses into "I straight up just
  stop playing the game" only after repeated re-encounters (P11, p.11).
- **Four behavioral responses**, in escalating order (p.11–12): (1) reduce
  emotional intensity of play (rushing/skipping to "get it over and done
  with" — the paper's title quote, P3); (2) adapt in-game behavior
  (avoiding certain content, switching group/role); (3) disengage
  (stepping away, taking breaks, deprioritizing); (4) quit entirely.
- **Extrinsic motivation can override the above three categories** (p.12–13):
  desire to see the game/world beyond the current obstacle (most prevalent —
  the *Zelda* Water Temple example, "no one likes that shit... but the rest
  of the game is beyond this"), identifying as a fan/franchise obligation
  (finishing a disliked *Final Fantasy XIII* "to fulfil my duty as a fan"),
  and rewards/achievements (collectible-hunting in *Subway Surfers* despite
  frustration, because of the anticipated prize).
- Existing quantitative need-frustration instruments (a modified Basic
  Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale, Chen et al. 2015)
  **lack content validity for games**: items don't capture perceived
  unfairness, meaningless choices, constrained playstyle, or
  disconnection-from-world experiences that interviewees spontaneously
  reported (p.13–14).
- Design-implication examples the authors offer directly (not tested, but
  argued from the model): *God of War Ragnarök*'s allies auto-solving
  puzzles after a short delay is legible as a designed trade-off — reduced
  autonomy satisfaction to avert competence frustration/stagnation in
  players who are "truly stuck" (p.13); *Candy Crush Saga*'s explicit
  up-front difficulty labeling per level may reduce felt frustration on
  repeated failure by setting the expectation in advance (p.16).
- Sample and saturation (p.6–7, Table 2): 12 players (ages 18–42, Prolific +
  snowball, UK/Portugal/Spain/Chile/Canada/Poland/South Africa/Netherlands),
  interviews 39–78 min (110,000 words total). Need-frustration categories
  saturated at interview 10; the expectations–behavior loop saturated at
  interview 12. Sole coder was the first author; the second author acted as
  an "auditor" (not independent co-coding) per constructivist grounded
  theory's rejection of inter-rater reliability as a validity guarantee
  (Charmaz 2006; Cornish et al. 2014, p.5) — the model was also member-checked
  against two interviewees who confirmed it fit their experience (p.5).

## Methods

Constructivist grounded theory (Charmaz 2006) on semi-structured,
~1-hour, critical-incident interviews (Zoom, Feb 2021–May 2022), theoretical
sampling for demographic/genre variety, three coding cycles (initial
incident-by-incident → focused → theoretical/selective), coded in MaxQDA.
Interview prompts scaffolded from the SDT handbook (Ryan & Deci 2017) and
the Chen et al. (2015) Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration
Scale wording, but responses were only coded as need frustration if
participants clearly described feeling controlled/coerced, failure/
self-doubt, or disconnection/exclusion — not merely negative affect. Two
positionality statements are included (both authors disclose SDT priors and
demographic blind spots) as the paper's substitute for inter-rater
reliability. This is theory-*generating*, explicitly not theory-*testing*:
no hypotheses are statistically tested, no effect sizes are reported.

## Results

- Qualitative frequency claim only ("almost every interviewee easily
  recalled instances of autonomy, competence, and relatedness frustration")
  — no counts, proportions, or per-category prevalence are reported; this is
  a categorical/structural model, not a quantified one.
- All three need-frustration types were empirically distinguishable by
  distinct triggering situations and (unquantified) distinct "emotional
  signatures," though the paper explicitly declines to detail the emotion
  categories for space/saturation reasons (p.7–8) — full coding tree is in
  supplementary OSF materials, not reproduced in the paper itself.
- No quantitative outcome measures (retention, session length, purchase,
  self-report enjoyment scales) were collected; all behavioral claims
  (rush/adapt/disengage/quit; extrinsic override) are retrospective
  self-report from interview, not observed play telemetry.

## Critique / open questions

- **N=12, single-coder-plus-auditor, Global-North-skewed, console/PC-only**
  (no mobile-primary player in the "favorite games" list despite mobile
  games being discussed) — the authors' own limitations section (p.16)
  flags this; treat the nine situation categories as a plausible, saturated
  *typology* from a narrow sample, not a population-representative taxonomy.
- Genuinely novel and useful move: separating *felt* frustration (event
  vs. expectation delta) from *raw* frustrating events reframes difficulty/
  fairness/agency design as an expectation-setting problem, not just a
  tuning problem. This directly extends, rather than merely restates, PENS/
  SDT.
- The "unfair situations" category's outward-blame signature is the
  **mirror image** of juul2013art's self-attribution finding used for rubric
  1.3/3.3 ("every failure teaches a specific lesson; players blame
  themselves and are right to") — this paper gives the qualitative mechanism
  for *when self-attribution breaks down* (perceived AI/opponent
  illegitimacy), which 1.3/3.3 don't currently name as a failure mode.
- No causal or experimental evidence — it cannot support effect-size or
  weight claims, only mechanism and category claims. Per this project's own
  evidence tiers (`design-evidence-quality.md`), this is **E3**: peer-reviewed
  (ACM CHI PLAY '23) small-N qualitative/observational — same tier as
  juul2013art and sweetser2005gameflow, above pure designer-opinion (E4/E5),
  below the PXI/PENS validated-instrument tier (E2).
- The Candy Crush and God of War design-implication examples are the
  authors' post-hoc interpretive illustrations, not tested against those
  games' actual player data — flag as designer-plausible-inference, not
  finding.

## Follow-up

- **Relevance: 4** — one-line justification: doesn't seed a wholly new
  rubric dimension, but (a) supplies a mechanism (expectation-violation)
  currently *absent* from the rubric's set-up/scoring logic that cuts
  across G2, 3.1, 3.2, 5.2, and 8.1; (b) gives concrete, quotable failure
  modes for dimension 2 (autonomy) and the outward-blame failure mode for
  1.3/3.3; (c) is the strongest available source, alongside juul2013art, for
  *why* failure cost calibration (3.2) and setback punishment (8.4) matter
  psychologically, not just as friction.
- Worth chasing: Melhárt (2018) and Deterding et al. (2022) on predictive
  processing applied to play, cited here (p.15) as the theoretical grounding
  for the expectation-delta mechanism — could be a stronger primary source
  for a future "expectation calibration" concept/criterion than this paper
  alone.
- Worth chasing: Kosa & Uysal (2021) and Pusey et al. (2021), the
  quantitative correlational precursors this paper triangulates with —
  would give an E2-tier companion source if their instruments and effect
  sizes are usable.

## Rubric implications

- **Supports 1.3 / 3.3** (feedback lets the model update; self-attribution
  of failure): adds a named failure mode — "unfair situations" competence
  frustration — where players attribute failure *outward* (game/opponent)
  rather than to themselves, specifically when AI/opponents are perceived
  as illegitimately advantaged. This is the mechanism by which 1.3/3.3's
  top anchor ("players blame themselves and are right to") fails; worth a
  one-line caution added to 3.3's anchor text citing this source.
- **Supports 2.2 / 2.4** (trade-offs not puzzles; multiple valid approaches):
  the *Outer Worlds* "meaningless choices" example is a concrete, quotable
  instance of illusory agency (branching dialogue converging to one
  outcome) — good citable evidence for why 2.2's anchor requires *felt*
  consequence, not just choice presence.
- **Supports 3.2 / 8.4** (failure cost calibration / setback punishment):
  the four-step disengagement ladder (reduce intensity → adapt → disengage →
  quit) gives an empirically-derived behavioral sequence that could become
  explicit 0–4 anchor language for 3.2 (e.g., "0 = players report wanting to
  quit; 4 = players self-report rushing/skipping only, never disengaging").
- **Supports 3.5** (session shape / "one more" hook): the three extrinsic-
  motivation categories (curiosity beyond the obstacle, fan identity,
  rewards/achievements) are exactly the forces that sustain a "one more"
  push through a frustrating segment — could be cited alongside
  cook2007chemistry for 3.5.
- **New criterion proposed — "Expectation calibration"** (candidate 8.6,
  dimension 8 Clarity & friction, or a new cross-cutting S-step alongside
  S1–S3): the rubric currently has no criterion for whether the game
  signals upcoming difficulty/constraint/randomness so player expectations
  track what the mechanic will actually do (Candy Crush's up-front
  difficulty labeling, p.16, is the paper's own example). Justification:
  this paper's central finding is that felt frustration is a function of
  the expectation–event *delta*, not event valence alone — a design lever
  entirely orthogonal to tuning difficulty/failure-cost itself, and not
  currently scored anywhere in v0.2. Proposed evidence tier: **E3** (same
  as source). Flag as a proposal only — not applied to `docs/rubric.md` by
  this note.
- **No weight-change evidence**: this is qualitative/theory-generating, so
  it cannot and does not supply an effect size or relative-importance
  number for any dimension weight — it should inform criterion *anchors*
  and *new criteria*, not the weight column.
