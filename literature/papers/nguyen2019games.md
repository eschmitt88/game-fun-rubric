# Games and the Art of Agency

**Type note:** this is analytic philosophy, not empirical psychology. Zero
data, zero playtests, zero surveys — the method throughout is conceptual
argument via thought experiment (racquetball with a spouse, charades,
Monopoly, *Super Mario Bros.*, chess, rock climbing vs. dancing). It is the
journal precis for Nguyen's 2020 OUP book *Games: Agency as Art*, the
primary source this brief was scoped around (the book itself was not
directly fetchable; this is the peer-reviewed article version of its core
argument, pages 423–462 of *Phil Review* 128(4), DOI
10.1215/00318108-7697863). Fetched via the *Philosophers' Annual* 2019
reprint mirror (pgrim.org) after PhilPapers/PhilArchive blocked direct
retrieval (Cloudflare 403) — the file is the actual Duke UP typeset PDF
(page headers/footers show "Downloaded from
read.dukeupress.edu/.../423nguyen.pdf"), i.e. full text, not an abstract
substitute. 74 citations (Semantic Scholar) in ~7 years, in one of the top
two general philosophy journals — very high credibility as *argument*, but
credibility here means "rigorous, influential philosophical position," not
"tested claim." Treat every claim below as **E4 at best** (theoretical
framework from a primary, authoritative source) — it never rises to E1–E3
because there is no data to rise from.

## TL;DR

Nguyen argues games are not a trivial or purely instrumental activity but a
distinctive **art form whose medium is agency itself**. Game designers
don't just build worlds; they specify goals, permitted abilities, and an
obstacle-laden "practical environment" — an *agential skeleton* — that the
player voluntarily inhabits. Doing so requires a real and non-obvious
psychological capacity: **striving play**, in which a player temporarily
adopts an intrinsically-arbitrary in-game goal ("disposable end") *as if*
it were a final end, precisely in order to have the experience of
struggling toward it. This is distinct from **achievement play** (playing
because you want the win, or what follows from it). The capacity is
explained via **layered agency**: a temporary, goal-committed sub-agency
nested inside and still monitored/regulated by the player's stable
overall agency (you can always "step back and ask: is this still fun?").
Games, on this view, are a technology for inscribing and communicating
modes of agency — "games are the art of agency," and playing widely is
"yoga for your agency."

## Claims

- **Two orientations toward Suitsian (rule-bound, goal-directed) play**:
  *achievement play* pursues the win itself or what follows from it;
  *striving play* temporarily acquires an interest in winning **for the
  sake of** the struggle, and the two are motivationally orthogonal, not
  points on the intrinsic/extrinsic axis (p. 428). Evidence: the
  racquetball-with-spouse case — declining free skill-improving lessons is
  rational only under striving play (pp. 428–29).
- **"Stupid games"** (bag-on-the-head, Twister, drinking games) are fun
  only if you try to win, and the funniest part is failure — proof that
  achievement players *cannot* properly play them; only striving players
  can (p. 429).
- **Disposable ends are genuine ends, not pseudo-ends**, argued against
  three skeptical readings in turn: (1) *pretense* — unlike a con artist's
  faked love (the Gaslight example), the striving player's in-game goal
  actually, causally guides choices, not just a cover story (pp. 447–48);
  (2) *make-believe* — unlike Walton's fictional "quasi-fear," disposable
  ends directly regulate real, nonfictional action (running toward stumps,
  not away from them, in a game where make-believe *fear* would run the
  other way) (pp. 448–51); (3) *bad instrumental desire* (via Millgram's
  "desiring-at-pill" argument) — striving players' desire-to-win is
  inferentially embedded (it actually generates action, unlike a
  car-salesman's manufactured product enthusiasm), so Millgram's argument
  against instrumentally-acquired desires doesn't apply (pp. 451–55).
- **Layered agency**: the primary/enduring agency doesn't dissolve during
  play, it recedes into a background monitoring role and can reassert
  itself (a fire alarm, a friend's text, "is this still fun?") (pp.
  443–44). This is offered as a general model for other "role"
  activities — professor, lawyer, judge — not unique to games (p. 445).
- **Voluntary, moderate agential disunity is a competence, not a
  pathology** — argued directly against Millgram's "unity constraint"
  picture of ideal agency, where any departure from unified ends is a
  vice (weakness of will, absentmindedness). Nguyen: game players
  routinely and rationally *choose* to seal off parts of their ends
  (e.g., forgetting "I want my spouse to have fun too" during a
  competitive match, precisely to deliver that fun) (pp. 444–46).
- **Disinterested interestedness**: aesthetic striving play answers the
  Kantian-disinterestedness worry about whether goal-pursuing activity
  can be aesthetic — in-game the player is fully interested (pursuing the
  win); stepping back, they are disinterested (they don't care whether
  they actually win, only about the quality of the struggle) (pp.
  439–40).
- **Harmony of self and challenge**: a distinctive, player-only aesthetic
  experience — not just that a solution is elegant (available to
  spectators too, e.g. an elegant chess move), but that *one's own
  abilities precisely fit the situation's demands* (p. 430). Contrast
  case: free dancing is an aesthetic proprioceptive experience but *not
  essentially a game* (no obstacle); rock climbing is essentially a game
  (the rock imposes real, unnecessary-but-adopted obstacles) — "climbing
  aims at overcoming an obstacle... dancing may occasionally be a game,
  but climbing essentially is one" (p. 431).
- **The artistic medium of "aesthetic striving games"** is not simply
  rules, nor simply the practical/physical environment, but the
  *combination*: goals + permitted abilities + practical environment,
  which together designate a temporary agency for the player to inhabit
  (pp. 433–38, culminating "games are the art of agency," p. 438).
- **Difficulty is not the point** (direct rebuttal of Hurka 2006): under
  the aesthetic-striving account, harder isn't automatically better —
  what matters is the aesthetic quality of the resulting fit/harmony, and
  *less* difficult games can be worthwhile for other aesthetic qualities,
  e.g. those "associated with power and ease" (p. 433).
- **Functional beauty is crisper in games than in ordinary life** because
  a game's goal is stipulated and unambiguous, whereas an artifact's
  "proper function" outside a game is often epistemically blurry
  (Parsons & Carlson's functional-beauty theory, applied) — "games are a
  teleologically crisper context" (pp. 456–57).
- **A pluralism of play, not a complete definition**: Nguyen explicitly
  rejects Suits's claim to have defined "game" completely, following
  Caillois — Suitsian obstacle-play and fictive/imaginative make-believe
  play (narrative tabletop RPGs like *Fiasco*, *The Stanley Parable*) are
  two distinct forms of play, and this paper only analyzes the first (p.
  427). *Sign* (the opening example — a LARP about deaf children
  spontaneously inventing sign language) is offered as showing the
  Suitsian, instrumental-task side of play can dominate even a
  fiction-heavy game's peak experience (pp. 423–25).
- **Winning and fictional success can come apart** (via Juul's
  fiction/reality hybrid model of video games): a player can care about
  the nonfictional fact of winning entirely independent of the fictional
  content (a high-score chaser who "doesn't care about the fiction at
  all," p. 451), and some games' win-conditions actively contradict their
  fiction (*Fiasco*'s rules reward playing your character's interests
  *against* their own good, to generate comic disaster) (pp. 450–51).

## Methods

Pure philosophical argument: definitional analysis (building on Suits's
*The Grasshopper* and Caillois's *Man, Play, and Games*), thought
experiments, and engagement with the aesthetics-of-sport and
philosophy-of-mind/practical-reason literatures (Millgram on desire and
agential unity, Walton on make-believe, Parsons & Carlson on functional
beauty). No games were studied empirically; all game examples (*Sign*,
*Super Mario Bros.*, chess, *Twister*, *Monopoly*, *Fiasco*, charades,
*PewPewPewPewPewPewPewPew*, artificial rock walls) are used as
illustrative cases for conceptual points, not as data.

## Results

Not applicable in the empirical sense — the paper's "results" are
philosophical distinctions and an argument structure:
achievement-vs-striving play → disposable ends are genuine → layered
agency explains how disposability is psychologically possible →
aesthetic striving play resolves the disinterestedness worry → games'
artistic medium is agency itself → games form a communicable "library of
agencies."

## Critique / open questions

- **Zero empirical grounding, by design.** This cannot adjudicate any of
  the rubric's live empirical questions (does difficulty matter, does
  juice help, does DDA work) — Nguyen's own rebuttal of Hurka
  ("difficulty is not the point") is a *philosophical* claim about what
  makes striving valuable, not a claim about what makes striving
  *enjoyable* or what predicts retention; it should not be read as
  corroborating or undercutting caroux2023player's null pooled-difficulty
  finding. Different questions entirely (aesthetic value vs. hedonic
  enjoyment vs. behavioural retention).
- **The paper explicitly is not about fun.** Nguyen frames the payoff as
  *aesthetic experience of one's own agency*, not enjoyment per se — a
  third category alongside the rubric's existing enjoyment/appreciation
  split (oliver2016video). This note's rubric-implications section below
  treats that as the single most load-bearing point for this project.
- **Internal tension worth flagging**: Nguyen argues stupid games
  (Twister, bag-on-the-head) require striving play, yet these are
  paradigmatically *toy-like*, low-stakes, silly activities — this cuts
  against any easy mapping of "striving play" onto "serious/high-effort
  play" and "achievement play" onto "casual." The taxonomy is orthogonal
  to intensity, not aligned with it (explicitly stated, p. 428).
  Designers should not assume "striving-play design" means "hardcore."
- **No account of failure modes** — the paper doesn't address what
  happens when disposable-end adoption *fails* (a player who can't get
  absorbed, who stays achievement-oriented against the designer's
  striving-play intent, or who defensively refuses to submerge). That
  gap matters for a rubric aimed at practical design guidance: Nguyen
  explains the phenomenon when it works, not how to reliably induce it or
  what to do when a playtester won't submerge.
- **"Layered agency" as monitoring** is asserted, not evidenced — the
  claim that players retain background capacity to "step back and ask: is
  this still fun?" is intuitively plausible and matches session-shape
  observations elsewhere in this project (ballou2023just's rush → adapt →
  disengage → quit ladder is a behavioural analogue), but Nguyen offers
  no data on how reliably or how often that monitoring actually fires —
  it's consistent with, not confirmatory of, ballou2023just's E3
  observational ladder.
- Highly technical/discursive philosophy prose; several sections
  (Millgram desire-acquisition, disinterestedness/Kant) are
  argument-internal to analytic philosophy debates with limited direct
  design applicability, included here for completeness but low
  design-actionability.

## Rubric implications

- **G2 (Interesting decisions exist)** — *adds* philosophical grounding,
  doesn't raise the tier (stays E5/E4-mixed). Nguyen's disposable-ends
  argument explains *why* an admittedly-arbitrary goal ("get the ball
  through the stupid little basket") can motivationally function as a
  real end during play — this is the mechanism underneath Burgun's
  "blind guess ↔ solved line" and Meier's "interesting decisions" claims
  already cited at G2. Cite alongside burgun2015why/meier2012interesting
  as the philosophical account of *why* arbitrary stakes work at all.
- **Dimension 2, framing (Agency & meaningful choice, 15%)** — *adds*,
  and suggests the dimension is currently framed too narrowly. Nguyen's
  central claim — designers sculpt goals + abilities + practical
  environment into a coherent "agential skeleton," and this whole
  package (not just decision density/2.1 or trade-off weight/2.2) is
  what players experience as agency — argues for a **proposed new
  criterion 2.6, "Distinctive, coherent agency"**: does the game's
  combination of goals/abilities/constraints feel like a specific,
  recognizable *mode of being* distinct from other games' (the way
  *Portal*'s wormhole-gun or chess's piece-movement rules do), rather
  than a generic decision tree? Justification: this is the paper's
  entire thesis and is currently uncaptured — 2.4 (multiple valid
  approaches) gestures at it but measures breadth of solutions, not
  distinctiveness of the agency itself. Tier would be E4 (single
  philosophical source, no independent corroboration yet).
- **1.5 (No dominant strategy, E5 burgun2015why)** — *adds* a second,
  independent (philosophy vs. designer-blog) source for the same
  intuition — genuine striving requires genuine uncertainty about the
  outcome, or the "struggle" collapses. Does not raise 1.5's tier past
  E4 (still no data), but reduces single-source risk.
- **4.3 (Weight and physicality — "toy test")** — *adds* a precise
  citation and a caution. Nguyen's climbing-vs-dancing distinction
  (climbing "essentially is a game," free dancing is aesthetic but not
  essentially one) sharpens what 4.3's "toy test" is actually checking:
  physicality/weight that's pleasurable *with no goal at all* is
  necessary-but-not-sufficient for *striving-play* value specifically
  because the obstacle/goal structure is what makes it a game rather than
  free movement. Recommend citing nguyen2019games at 4.3 alongside the
  existing burgun2015why E5 caution.
- **3.1/3.3 (challenge–skill match, sense of control)** — *adds*, does
  not contradict. "Harmony of self and challenge" (p. 430–31) is a
  philosophically-framed restatement of flow's challenge/skill match,
  independently arrived at from aesthetics rather than psychology —
  corroborating, not confirmatory (E4, cannot raise vandenabeele2020development's
  E2 or chen2007flow's tier).
- **Known gap "Toys vs tools" (Malone §5)** — this paper *reframes* the
  gap rather than closing it. Nguyen's trichotomy — achievement play
  (goal wanted for itself or its payoff), striving play (goal temporarily
  and disposably adopted for the struggle), and fictive/make-believe
  play (Caillois's other pole, no real obstacles) — is a cleaner,
  independently-motivated cut than Malone's "means to an external goal"
  framing already flagged as missing. **Recommend**: update the Known
  Gaps bullet to cite nguyen2019games alongside Malone, and note that the
  rubric's target-motivation-profile setup (S1) implicitly assumes
  achievement-style play (yee2015handy's motivations are largely
  achievement-flavored); a game explicitly designed for striving play
  (Nguyen's "aesthetic striving games") might need every criterion
  re-read through "does this deliver a good *struggle*" rather than
  "does this deliver a good *win*" — a distinct lens from anything
  currently in S1–S3.
- **Top framing paragraph ("measures fun... not meaning")** — *adds* a
  third candidate outcome the rubric doesn't currently name: aesthetic
  experience of one's own well-fitted agency, distinct from both
  hedonic enjoyment (oliver2016video's gameplay→competence/autonomy→
  enjoyment path) and narrative appreciation
  (oliver2016video's story→relatedness/insight→appreciation path).
  Nguyen would likely deny that "harmony of self and challenge" reduces
  to either. **Proposed**, low-confidence: this may warrant a documented
  caveat rather than a scored criterion — the rubric measures fun, and
  Nguyen's aesthetic-striving payoff is adjacent to but not identical
  with fun (a hard-won, satisfying chess move can be aesthetically
  valuable while barely registering as "fun" in the PXI/PENS
  self-report sense). No new criterion proposed; flag as a documented
  limitation of "fun" as the sole target construct.
- **No change recommended to weights.** Nothing here is quantitative;
  it cannot move any of the provisional 5/10/15/20% weights.

**Relation to existing notes**: complements burgun2015why (1.5, G2) and
meier2012interesting (G2, dimension 2) as a third, independently-sourced,
much higher-credibility account of why arbitrary in-game goals motivate —
but unlike those two, offers zero empirical or even practitioner-shipped
evidence, so it should be read as *conceptual scaffolding*, not as tier-
raising evidence. Also connects to koster2012theory10yearslater's
mastery/fun framing (both treat play as psychologically distinctive from
ordinary practical activity) and to deterding2015joys's
functional/psychosocial and solitary-play framing (both discuss what
players do with/without their "usual" ends active during play).
