# The Gamer Motivation Model in Handy Reference Chart and Slides

*by Nick Yee, published December 15th, 2015, quanticfoundry.com*
(Categories: Analytics. 7 Comments at time of capture.)

A total of over 140,000 gamers worldwide have now completed the Gamer
Motivation Profile. Statistical analysis of how motivations cluster
together is consistent with what we reported earlier.

Want to know how you compare with other gamers on these motivations?
Take a 5-minute survey and get your Gamer Motivation Profile.

To make the Gamer Motivation Model easier to understand and share,
we've put together some reference charts and slides. First, we have a
chart that lists all the motivations and how they are related.
Motivations in the same column are more highly correlated than
motivations in different columns. The chart also provides brief
descriptions of each motivation.

Second, we have a short set of PDF slides that provides the chart and
more detailed descriptions of each motivation along with game
examples. Click the image below to download the PDF.

The image asset itself was not separately located/verified.]

We have some really exciting findings we'll be sharing in the coming
weeks (e.g., a higher-level visual map of all these motivations), but
we wanted to make sure we laid the groundwork for describing the model
before diving too deep in the data.

**About the Author: Nick Yee** — Nick is the co-founder and analytics
lead of Quantic Foundry. He combines social science and data science to
understand gamer behavior in large-scale game data.

## Supplementary source 1 — "How We Created the Gamer Motivation
Profile" (2015-06-18), full text as fetched

**Super short version** (author's own summary): "We generated an
initial inventory based on a literature review of existing models,
tested it with 1,127 gamers, validated it with another 600 gamers, and
used factor analysis to identify 5 groups of motivations."

Method: catalogued motivations from existing frameworks (Bartle's
Player Types, LeBlanc's Aesthetics [MDA], Lazzaro's Fun Types, Sherry's
Gaming Uses & Gratifications), wrote ~50 inventory items (5-point
importance scale), piloted on 1,127 gamers (primarily MMO players),
replicated on 600 gamers from "a more representative gamer panel,"
applied Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA).

Result — v1 model, 5 groups / 11 motivations:
- **Action**: Destruction (chaos, mayhem, guns, explosives),
  Excitement (fast-paced, intense, adrenaline)
- **Strategy**: Mastery (skill, overcoming difficult challenges),
  Planning (careful decision-making, strategic thinking)
- **Achievement**: Completion (finish every mission/collectible),
  Power (becoming powerful in-game)
- **Social**: Competition (dueling/matches with other players),
  Community (interacting/collaborating with others)
- **Immersion**: Customization (expression, deep customization),
  Fantasy (becoming someone/somewhere else), Story (elaborate
  storyline, characters)

Notable methodological observations in the post: Competition clustered
with Social (collaboration) in this data, not with Achievement as in
an earlier MMO-only model — attributed to a shift from MUD-era
domination-PvP toward contemporary team-based competition. No distinct
Exploration factor emerged (items loaded onto Fantasy or Mastery
instead). Diversion/Escapism items were tested and formed their own
factor but were excluded from the framework as "reasons for seeking
out entertainment, but not specific reasons for playing video games
per se."

## Supplementary source 2 — "How We Developed The Gamer Motivation
Profile v2" (2015-07-20), full text as fetched

**Super short version**: "We validated and refined our motivations
model based on data from 30,000 gamers around the world."

Sample composition at N=30,000+: US+Canada 6,222; Brazil 6,044;
Indonesia 6,000; Philippines 3,198; Singapore 2,316; EU 2,004;
Australia 660; East Asia 510; plus a long tail of other countries.

Method: factor analysis run separately on US+Canada, Indonesia, and EU
sub-samples — "the number of factors that emerged and the factor
composition across these 3 regions were identical" — and again on the
full pooled dataset, with the same result. Hierarchical clustering was
then applied to the 12 resulting motivation factors to build a
dendrogram of how they relate.

Result — v2 model (this is the model the Dec-2015 "handy reference"
chart depicts), 12 motivations in 6 clusters, clusters in 3
higher-order branches:

- **Immersion** cluster: Fantasy (become someone/somewhere else),
  Story (elaborate storyline, interesting characters)
- **Creativity** cluster (new in v2): Design (expression, deep
  customization — renamed from "Customization"), Discovery (explore,
  tinker, experiment — new factor, most closely related to Design)
- **Action** cluster: Destruction (chaos, mayhem, guns, explosives),
  Excitement (fast-paced, intense, adrenaline)
- **Social** cluster: Competition (dueling/matches — note: text of the
  post itself mislabels this "Completion" once, but the axis is
  competition per the definition given and per all later posts),
  Community (interacting/collaborating with others)
- **Mastery** cluster (renamed from "Strategy" after negative user
  reaction to a low "Strategy" score): Challenge (skill, overcoming
  difficult challenges — renamed from "Mastery" factor label),
  Strategy (careful decision-making, strategic thinking)
- **Achievement** cluster: Completion (finish every
  mission/collectible), Power (becoming powerful in-game)

Three higher-order branches from the dendrogram:
- **Immersion-Exploration** branch = Immersion + Creativity clusters
  (relating to story/game-world design)
- **Achievement-Mastery** branch = Achievement + Mastery clusters
  (progressing/attaining power)
- **Action-Social** branch = Action + Social clusters (energetic,
  gregarious modes of play) — explicitly linked by the author to the
  Big Five personality trait Extraversion (gregariousness,
  excitement-seeking, assertiveness, cheerfulness), citing Big Five
  research as external validation of this otherwise counterintuitive
  grouping.

Changes from v1 flagged explicitly by the author: (1) added Discovery
factor, forming new Creativity cluster with renamed Design factor; (2)
renamed "Strategy" cluster to "Mastery" for user-facing clarity (the
underlying Challenge/Strategy factors are unchanged).

## Supplementary source 3 — "7 Things We Learned About Primary Gaming
Motivations From Over 250,000 Gamers" (2016-12-15), full text as
fetched

Confirms the 12-motivation model is unchanged a year later, now at N =
"over 250,000 gamers worldwide." Defines "primary motivation" as each
gamer's single highest-scoring motivation (percentile-normalized
against the full dataset). Findings relevant to this project:

1. Men's most common primary motivations: Competition, Destruction;
   least common: Discovery, Power.
2. Women's most common: Completion, Fantasy; least common: Excitement,
   Challenge.
3. Distribution is far more even among men (max/min ratio 2.5×, i.e.
   14.1%/5.6%) than among women (5.7×, i.e. 17.0%/3.0%) — top-3
   motivations cover 36.2% of men vs. 47.7% of women.
4. Non-binary respondents (2,819 in the dataset, ~1.1% of sample):
   most common Fantasy, Design; least common Power, Excitement; most
   polarized of any segment (8.5× ratio; top-3 = 50.2%).
5. Youngest segment (13-25): Competition dominant, ~50% more frequent
   than 2nd place (Destruction).
6. 36+ segment: Competition drops from 1st to 9th place; Fantasy and
   Completion become most common. Cites an earlier post: "the appeal
   of Competition declines the most rapidly with age" of all 12
   motivations.
7. Completion is in the top 3 primary motivation across every
   demographic cut examined — framed by the author as "low-risk,
   high-reward" for broad-audience design (example given: Pokémon Go).
