## 3. Gamedeveloper.com recap of the GDC 2016 talk itself

URL: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/analyzing-why-people-play-games-and-how-it-changes-over-time
(fetched via WebFetch, full-article extraction; original trade-press
Gamasutra/Game Developer recap, written contemporaneously with the 2016 talk)

Key extracted content:

- **The Big Five (OCEAN) mapping**, as VandenBerghe presents it in this
  specific talk: **Openness to Experience → fantasy preferences.** Quote
  attributed to VandenBerghe: *"If you have a high imagination score you
  prefer fantasy, if you have a low score you prefer realism."* The other
  four traits are discussed in the talk per the recap but their specific
  domain-mapping is not spelled out in this article's text (the recap
  focuses on Openness as the worked example) — the full five-way mapping by
  name is instead documented in the earlier written 5-Domains material
  (source #4 below).
- **The player's journey — four stages** (as presented in this talk):
  1. **Discovery** — "I've heard of that game."
  2. **Evaluation** — "Yeah, I've tried that game."
  3. **Use** — "Oh yeah, I played that!"
  4. **Affinity** — players self-identify with the game (his example:
     someone calling themselves a "*Bloodborne* player," not just someone
     who played it).
- **The time-course claim (core thesis of this talk):** *"You don't keep
  playing for the same reasons you start playing — your taste changes."*
  And: *"the impact of 'taste satisfaction' is going to go down. The longer
  you play a game, the less you care if it matches your individual
  tastes."* — i.e., the Big-Five/5-Domains "taste" model predicts initial
  *appeal* (why you pick up a game) but its predictive power *decays* with
  continued play; something else has to take over to sustain engagement.
- **What sustains long-term play, per the talk: Self-Determination Theory
  (via Scot Rigby's PENS model), three universal motivations:**
  1. **Competence** — controlling outcomes, mastery.
  2. **Autonomy** — causal agency, self-expression.
  3. **Relatedness** — glossed in the talk (per this recap) as *"knowing
     where you fit into the world,"* explicitly **not** just social
     stimulation/multiplayer content.
- VandenBerghe frames the combined model (taste → SDT satisfaction →
  long-term experience/affinity/nostalgia) as a practical communication tool
  for dev teams to answer "who are our players, what do they want" early in
  production, and to know which stage of the journey a given feature is
  meant to serve.

## 4. Peachpit.com — "Fundamentals of Game Design: Understanding Your
   Player | VandenBerghe's Five Domains of Play"

URL: https://www.peachpit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=2168935
(an excerpt/companion article for Fulton & Schell's *Fundamentals of Game
Design* / the Game Design Workshop tradition, summarizing VandenBerghe's
written 5-Domains framework as first presented at GDC 2012 ("The 5 Domains
of Play: Applying Psychology's Big 5 Motivation Domains to Games") and GDC
2013 ("Acting Like Players: Applying the 5 Domains of Play") — the earlier,
written-up version of the model the brief asked to also draw on.)

Key extracted content — **the five domains, named, each mapped to one Big
Five trait, each described as a continuum/sliding scale (not a binary
type), with player preference framed as shifting by mood/circumstance**:

1. **Novelty ↔ Openness to Experience.** High: "games that include a lot of
   variety and unexpected elements." Low: familiarity/comfort (e.g., word
   games over complex sci-fi).
2. **Challenge ↔ Conscientiousness.** "Effort and control." High: difficult
   games requiring precision. Low: sandbox environments, exploration without
   mandatory objectives.
3. **Stimulation ↔ Extraversion.** High: social engagement, party games,
   playing with/against others. Low: solo experiences.
4. **Harmony ↔ Agreeableness.** "Social harmony" — cooperative play. Low
   end: strictly competitive conflict (example given: *Street Fighter*).
5. **Threat ↔ Neuroticism.** Explicitly flagged as counter-intuitive:
   *"players' reactions to it are the opposite of what you might expect"* —
   players **high** in neuroticism are drawn **toward** threatening/
   frightening content (e.g., survival horror), not away from it.

Additional framing from this article: each domain is a "continuum, sliding
scale," not a binary quality-you-have-or-don't; VandenBerghe's own quoted
framing of intent: *"This model is not about making better game design;
this model is about making better game designers ... these designers will
internalize these models and make better decisions, because they will
understand their players better."* (this specific quote is also corroborated
independently by the general web-search summary pass, attributed to the
2012 GDC talk).
